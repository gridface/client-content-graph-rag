# CHAPTER 9: STRIPING, MARKING, AND ACCESSIBILITY

You've done the hard work—the grading, the base installation, the paving, the compaction. The asphalt is smooth, properly sloped for drainage, and ready to handle traffic for the next 20 years. But here's the thing: the job's not finished until the lines are down. In fact, for many of your customers, the striping is what they see first and remember most. Faded lines, crooked parking stalls, or missing accessible parking markings don't just look unprofessional—they can expose your customer to liability and cost you callbacks and referrals.

This chapter isn't about becoming a striping specialist (though some of you might want to add that service). It's about understanding pavement marking standards, knowing when striping can happen, ensuring ADA compliance for your customers, and recognizing opportunities to either partner with striping contractors or offer the service yourself. Whether you're paving a parking lot, a subdivision road, or a commercial driveway, understanding striping requirements helps you deliver a complete, compliant project that keeps customers happy and coming back.

---

## 9.1 Pavement Marking Standards

Pavement markings aren't just paint on asphalt—they're a communication system. They tell drivers where to go, where to stop, and where they're allowed to park. They improve safety, manage traffic flow, and when done right, make a property look organized and professional. Understanding marking standards helps you coordinate with striping contractors, answer customer questions, and avoid costly mistakes.

### Material Types

Not all pavement marking materials are created equal, and the right choice depends on traffic volume, climate, budget, and expected service life. Here's what you need to know about the main options:

#### Traffic Paint

**What it is**: Water-based or solvent-based paint specifically formulated for pavement marking. This is your basic, economical option.

**Advantages**:
- Lowest initial cost (typically $0.05-0.15 per linear foot for standard 4-inch lines)
- Easy to apply with spray equipment
- Fast drying (often 5-15 minutes)
- Good for low-traffic areas
- Widely available

**Disadvantages**:
- Shortest service life (6-12 months in moderate traffic)
- Lower durability and wear resistance
- Reduced reflectivity compared to premium materials
- Requires frequent restriping

**Best Applications**: Parking lots with light traffic, residential streets, temporary markings, budget-conscious projects where annual restriping is acceptable.

**Pro Tip**: If your customer is on a tight budget but you know they'll need to restripe annually anyway, traffic paint is a defensible choice. Just make sure they understand the maintenance requirement upfront—don't let it be a surprise.

#### Thermoplastic

**What it is**: A hot-applied plastic material (typically heated to 400-450°F) that bonds to the pavement and hardens as it cools. Think of it as melting plastic onto the pavement.

**Advantages**:
- Excellent durability (3-6 years in high traffic)
- Superior reflectivity with embedded glass beads
- Resistant to wear from traffic and snowplows
- Lower life-cycle cost despite higher initial cost
- Available in multiple colors and patterns

**Disadvantages**:
- Higher initial cost ($0.50-2.00+ per linear foot)
- Requires specialized equipment (melter/applicator)
- Application skill required to avoid defects
- Less practical for small projects

**Best Applications**: High-traffic roadways, crosswalks, stop bars, highway markings, any location requiring long service life and high visibility.

**Customer Value Proposition**: "Yes, thermoplastic costs more upfront, but over five years you'll save money compared to repainting every year. Plus, it's safer—the reflectivity stays bright even in wet conditions."

#### Preformed Tape

**What it is**: Factory-manufactured marking tape with pressure-sensitive adhesive backing. Essentially, it's like giant reflective tape that sticks to the pavement.

**Advantages**:
- Very long service life (5-8 years)
- Extremely high reflectivity
- Consistent line width and appearance
- No drying time required
- Can be applied in lower temperatures than paint
- Excellent for specialty markings (arrows, symbols, crosswalks)

**Disadvantages**:
- Highest initial cost ($2.00-5.00+ per linear foot for standard tape)
- Requires clean, dry, smooth surface
- Can be damaged by snowplows if not recessed
- May debond if surface prep is inadequate
- Not ideal for long continuous runs on tight budgets

**Best Applications**: Crosswalks, arrows, legends, symbols, accessible parking markings, areas requiring maximum durability and visibility.

**Pro Tip**: For ADA accessible parking spaces, preformed tape symbols look professional and last longer than paint. It's a small upgrade that makes a big impression.

#### Epoxy Systems

**What it is**: Two-component chemical systems (resin + hardener) that cure to form a durable, bonded marking. Think of it as industrial-grade adhesive with color and reflective properties.

**Advantages**:
- Excellent durability (2-4 years)
- Strong bond to pavement
- Good chemical resistance
- Can be applied thicker for enhanced reflectivity
- Good in high-temperature climates

**Disadvantages**:
- Moderate to high cost ($0.30-1.50 per linear foot)
- Requires mixing and has limited pot life
- Longer cure time than paint
- Temperature-sensitive application
- Skill required for proper mixing and application

**Best Applications**: Industrial areas, loading docks, areas exposed to chemicals or heavy traffic, warm climates where paint doesn't hold up.

### Color Standards and Applications

Pavement marking colors aren't arbitrary—they follow established standards from the Manual on Uniform Traffic Control Devices (MUTCD) and local regulations. Using the wrong color isn't just unprofessional; it can create confusion and liability for your customer.

#### White Markings

**Standard Uses**:
- **Lane lines**: Separate traffic flowing in the same direction
- **Edge lines**: Define the edge of the roadway or parking area
- **Stop bars**: Indicate where vehicles should stop at intersections or crosswalks
- **Crosswalks**: Pedestrian crossing areas
- **Parking stall lines**: Delineate individual parking spaces
- **Turn arrows and legends**: Direct traffic movement

**Why it matters**: White is the most common color you'll encounter. In a parking lot, virtually all the striping except accessible parking will be white.

**Customer Communication**: "All the parking stalls, aisle markings, and pedestrian crosswalks will be in white, which is the national standard and what drivers expect to see."

#### Yellow Markings

**Standard Uses**:
- **Centerlines**: Separate opposing traffic flows on two-way roads
- **No-passing zones**: Solid double yellow lines indicate passing is prohibited
- **Left-turn lane boundaries**: Separate turn lanes from through lanes
- **Median edges**: Define raised or painted medians
- **Curbs and obstacles**: Sometimes used for curb painting (check local codes)

**Why it matters**: Yellow means opposing traffic or a barrier. Using yellow incorrectly (like for parking stall lines) confuses drivers.

**Common Mistake**: Don't paint parking lot main aisles with yellow centerlines unless traffic actually flows in opposite directions on that aisle. If it's one-way, yellow isn't appropriate.

#### Blue Markings

**Standard Uses**:
- **Accessible parking spaces**: The international symbol of accessibility (wheelchair symbol) is painted or applied in blue within designated accessible spaces
- **Accessible aisle markings**: Some jurisdictions require blue diagonal hatching in access aisles

**Critical Point**: Blue markings for accessible parking are NOT optional—they're required by the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA). Missing or faded blue markings can result in fines and liability for property owners.

**Customer Protection**: "The blue accessible parking symbols are a federal requirement. We'll make sure they're clearly visible and compliant, which protects you from ADA complaints."

#### Red and Green Markings

**Standard Uses**:
- **Red**: Fire lanes (no parking), emergency vehicle access, sometimes used for curbs or restricted areas
- **Green**: Bike lanes (less common in parking lots), sometimes used for designated parking areas (permit zones, electric vehicle charging)

**Application Note**: Red and green uses vary by local jurisdiction. Always verify with local code or the engineer of record before using non-standard colors.

### Line Widths and Dimensions

Line width matters—not just for aesthetics, but for visibility, compliance with standards, and material quantity estimation.

#### Standard 4-Inch Width

**What it's for**: This is your baseline, standard line width for most applications.

**Uses**:
- Parking stall lines
- Standard lane lines on roadways
- Most parking lot aisle markings
- Edge lines in lower-traffic areas

**Why it matters**: 4-inch lines provide adequate visibility for drivers while minimizing material usage and cost. When someone says "stripe the parking lot," they're usually assuming 4-inch lines unless specified otherwise.

**Material Calculation**: For estimating purposes, a 4-inch line uses approximately 1 linear foot of coverage per linear foot of line. Your striping contractor will factor in material coverage rates based on the specific product.

#### 6-Inch Width for Emphasis Areas

**What it's for**: Enhanced visibility in critical locations.

**Uses**:
- Stop bars (at intersections, stop signs, crosswalks)
- Crosswalk lines (the parallel lines on either side)
- Fire lane markings
- Main traffic lanes on higher-speed roadways
- Areas where driver attention is critical

**Why it matters**: The wider line catches the eye better and signals "pay attention here." Stop bars need to be obvious, especially in wet or low-light conditions.

**Customer Value**: "We recommend 6-inch stop bars at your main entrance for better visibility and safety—it's a small cost increase that makes a big difference."

#### Edge Line Specifications

**What they are**: Lines that define the edge of the roadway or driving surface.

**Standard Practice**:
- Roadways: Typically 4-inch white lines along the right edge (direction of travel)
- Parking lots: Often optional unless specified, but can help define boundaries and improve appearance
- Width can increase to 6-inch or 8-inch on high-speed roads or where snow removal occurs

**Pro Tip**: Edge lines in parking lots aren't always required, but they look professional and help prevent drivers from cutting corners or driving off the pavement. Consider recommending them for finished appearance.

#### Double Line Spacing

**What it is**: When you need two parallel lines (such as double yellow centerlines or double white lines for no-passing zones).

**Standard Spacing**: 
- Double centerlines (yellow): Typically spaced 2-3 inches apart (measured between the closest edges)
- Double lines for restricted zones: 4-6 inches apart in some applications
- Always verify local standards

**Why it matters**: Double lines have specific meanings (no passing, restricted zones), and the spacing affects interpretation. Don't improvise—follow the spec.

## 9.2 Parking Lot Striping

This is where most asphalt contractors encounter striping requirements. A parking lot isn't finished until it's properly striped, and the layout directly affects property function, customer satisfaction, and legal compliance. Even if you don't do the striping yourself, you need to understand these requirements to coordinate work and advise your customers.

### Layout Design

Good parking lot layout makes efficient use of space, moves traffic safely, and meets code requirements. Poor layout creates bottlenecks, increases accidents, and wastes space. Here's what drives the design:

#### Stall Dimensions (9'×18' Typical)

**Standard Parking Space**: 
- **Width**: 9 feet (measured perpendicular to the aisle)
- **Length**: 18 feet (measured parallel to the aisle)
- **Total area**: 162 square feet per standard space

**Why these dimensions**: 
- 9 feet provides adequate room for vehicle doors to open and people to enter/exit
- 18 feet accommodates standard vehicles (sedans, SUVs, small trucks)
- These are consensus minimum dimensions; many codes require them

**Variations**:
- **Compact spaces**: 7.5-8 feet wide × 15-16 feet long (only allowed for a portion of spaces, typically 20-30%)
- **Oversized spaces**: 10 feet wide × 20 feet long (for trucks, luxury vehicles, or premium parking)
- **Parallel spaces**: Typically 8 feet wide × 22-24 feet long

**Customer Advice**: "The standard 9×18 stall fits most vehicles and meets code. If you want to offer compact spaces, we can do that for up to 30% of spaces, which allows you to fit more cars—but label them clearly as 'Compact Only.'"

**Pro Tip**: Tighter spaces (like 8.5 feet) might fit one more row of parking, but they create dings, customer complaints, and shopping cart damage. Unless space is extremely tight, stick with 9 feet—it's worth it.

#### Aisle Widths Based on Angle

The aisle width—the driving lane between rows of parking—depends on whether parking is perpendicular, angled, or parallel. This isn't arbitrary; it's geometry based on turning radius and backup distance.

**90-Degree (Perpendicular) Parking**:
- **One-way aisle**: 24 feet (minimum), 26 feet (preferred)
- **Two-way aisle**: 26 feet (minimum), 28-30 feet (preferred)
- **Why**: Cars need room to turn into spaces at 90 degrees and back out safely

**60-Degree Angled Parking**:
- **One-way aisle**: 18 feet (minimum), 20 feet (preferred)
- **Why**: The angle makes entry/exit easier; drivers can pull in and out at a shallower angle

**45-Degree Angled Parking**:
- **One-way aisle**: 13 feet (minimum), 15 feet (preferred)
- **Why**: Even easier entry/exit angle; often used where space is tight

**Parallel Parking**:
- **Aisle width**: 12-14 feet minimum for one-way, 20+ feet for two-way
- **Why**: Parallel spaces don't require much turning room, just enough to maneuver in and out

**Key Point**: Undersized aisles cause accidents, damage, and frustrated customers. Don't cut corners to squeeze in one more row of parking—it's not worth the liability.

**Bidding Tip**: If a customer's preliminary layout shows inadequate aisle widths, point it out. "I noticed the aisles are only 22 feet for 90-degree parking. That's tight and could cause issues. Can we bump it to 26 feet? It'll cost a bit more in paving, but it prevents problems."

#### Traffic Flow Patterns

**One-Way vs. Two-Way Aisles**:
- **One-way**: More efficient space usage, better traffic flow, fewer conflicts, can use angled parking
- **Two-way**: More flexible circulation, but requires wider aisles and typically uses 90-degree parking

**Directional Arrows**:
- Use arrows to direct traffic flow in one-way aisles
- Place arrows approximately every 50-100 feet
- Ensure arrows are visible and unambiguous

**Entry/Exit Considerations**:
- Separate entry and exit points when possible (reduces conflicts)
- Avoid placing entry/exit driveways directly across from each other on public roads (sight distance issues)
- Consider peak hour traffic flow

**Pedestrian Pathways**:
- Striped or marked pedestrian walkways from building entrances to parking areas
- Crosswalks at major pedestrian crossing points
- Clear pathways to accessible parking spaces

**Why This Matters to Your Customer**: "A well-designed traffic flow reduces accidents, speeds up parking turnover, and creates a better experience for customers. We can coordinate with your engineer to ensure the striping layout works with the paving design."

#### Fire Lanes and Emergency Access

Fire lanes are critical for code compliance and safety—and getting them wrong can delay occupancy permits or result in violations.

**What is a Fire Lane**:
- A designated access route for fire trucks and emergency vehicles
- Must remain clear of parked vehicles at all times
- Typically 20-26 feet wide (check local fire code)

**Marking Requirements**:
- Red curb or red pavement marking (depending on local code)
- "FIRE LANE - NO PARKING" text or stenciling
- Signage at intervals (typically every 50-100 feet)
- May require thermoplastic or durable materials for longevity

**Design Considerations**:
- Fire lanes must provide access to all building entrances
- Must accommodate fire truck turning radius (often 45-foot inside radius)
- Must be located within specified distance of buildings (typically 15-30 feet from structure)
- Surface must support fire truck loads (80,000+ lbs for aerial equipment)

**Coordination Point**: Fire lane locations should be determined during design, not during striping. The fire marshal must approve the layout before paving begins.

**Customer Protection**: "We'll coordinate with the fire marshal to ensure fire lanes are properly located and marked. This prevents delays in getting your occupancy permit and avoids violations later."

### Accessible Parking Requirements

This is non-negotiable. The Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) is federal law, and violations can result in lawsuits, fines, and bad publicity. Every parking lot serving the public or employees must have accessible parking spaces—no exceptions.

#### ADA Compliance

**Basic Requirements** (2010 ADA Standards for Accessible Design):

**Number of Spaces Required**:
- 1-25 total spaces: 1 accessible space required
- 26-50 spaces: 2 accessible spaces required
- 51-75 spaces: 3 accessible spaces required
- 76-100 spaces: 4 accessible spaces required
- For larger lots, approximately 1 accessible space per 25 total spaces (follow ADA table for exact requirements)

**Van-Accessible Spaces**:
- At least 1 in every 6 accessible spaces (rounded up) must be van-accessible
- Van-accessible spaces must have 8-foot minimum width access aisle (regular accessible spaces can have 5-foot aisle)

**Location Requirements**:
- Accessible spaces must be on the shortest accessible route to the building entrance
- Must be located on relatively level ground (max 2% slope in any direction)
- Cannot have excessive cross-slope in the access aisle or parking space
- Must connect to an accessible route (sidewalk) without requiring travel behind parked vehicles

**Surface Requirements**:
- Spaces and access aisles must be firm, stable, and slip-resistant
- Maximum slope: 1:48 (2%) in all directions
- No changes in level; smooth transitions to accessible routes

**Why This Matters**: "ADA compliance isn't optional. We'll make sure your accessible parking meets federal requirements—location, dimensions, slopes, and markings. This protects you legally and ensures everyone can access your property safely."

**Common Mistakes to Avoid**:
- Placing accessible spaces far from the entrance (they must be closest)
- Inadequate or incorrectly sloped access aisles
- Missing or faded pavement markings
- No vertical signage (required in addition to pavement markings)
- Van spaces without proper aisle width

#### Van-Accessible Spaces

Van-accessible spaces have additional requirements because they accommodate wheelchair lifts and ramps.

**Dimensions**:
- **Parking space width**: 8 feet minimum (can be standard 9 feet)
- **Access aisle width**: 8 feet minimum (wider than standard 5-foot aisle)
- **Length**: 18 feet minimum (standard parking length)
- **Total width**: 16 feet minimum for space plus aisle

**Marking and Signage**:
- Must include the International Symbol of Accessibility (ISA) painted in blue
- "VAN ACCESSIBLE" text on pavement or sign
- Vertical sign with "Van-Accessible" designation (required—pavement markings alone aren't sufficient)
- Sign height: 60 inches minimum to bottom of sign

**Why Van-Accessible Matters**: Wheelchair vans with side-entry lifts need the extra aisle width. Without proper van spaces, you're excluding a significant portion of people with disabilities.

**Pro Tip**: Some property owners don't understand the difference between standard accessible and van-accessible spaces. Explain it clearly: "Van-accessible spaces need a wider access aisle—8 feet instead of 5—because wheelchair lifts deploy from the side of the van."

#### Access Aisles

Access aisles are the marked zones adjacent to accessible parking spaces that must remain clear of vehicles and obstructions. They're as important as the parking space itself.

**Purpose**:
- Provide space for wheelchair users to exit vehicles
- Allow deployment of wheelchair lifts and ramps
- Connect accessible parking spaces to accessible routes

**Requirements**:
- **Width**: 5 feet minimum (60 inches) for standard accessible spaces; 8 feet minimum (96 inches) for van-accessible spaces
- **Length**: Full length of the parking space (18 feet minimum)
- **Marking**: Diagonal hatching (typically white or blue diagonal lines at 45 degrees, spaced 12-24 inches apart)
- **Surface**: Same slope and surface requirements as the parking space (max 2% in all directions)
- **Clearance**: No parking, no objects, no obstructions

**Two Spaces Can Share One Aisle**: 
- An 8-foot access aisle can be placed between two accessible parking spaces
- This is more efficient than providing separate aisles for each space
- The aisle serves both spaces, so only one access aisle is needed for each pair

**Common Violations**:
- Access aisles blocked by shopping carts, snow, debris, or parked cars
- Faded markings that don't clearly show the aisle
- Improper slope (water pooling, excessive cross-slope)
- Access aisle doesn't connect to an accessible route

**Customer Responsibility**: "The access aisle must stay clear—no parking, no storage, no planters. Make sure your maintenance staff and customers understand this. We'll mark it clearly with diagonal striping."

#### Signage Requirements

Pavement markings alone are NOT sufficient for ADA compliance—vertical signage is required.

**Minimum Signage Requirements**:
- **Each accessible space** must have a sign mounted at 60 inches minimum height (measured to bottom of sign)
- Sign must display the International Symbol of Accessibility (wheelchair symbol)
- Van-accessible spaces must include "Van Accessible" designation on sign
- Signs must be visible and not obstructed by parked vehicles

**Additional Signage Elements**:
- Some jurisdictions require "TOW AWAY" warnings or fine amounts
- May require permit or placard instructions
- Color and size standards vary by jurisdiction (check local code)

**Why Signage Matters**: Without proper signs, a space is NOT legally compliant, even if the pavement is perfectly marked. Faded signs or missing signs = violation.

**Coordination**: "Signage is typically handled by others, but we'll coordinate location and installation to ensure everything meets code."

### Application Timing

Here's a critical point that prevents callbacks and customer complaints: you can't stripe brand new asphalt immediately. The pavement needs time to cure, or the markings won't adhere properly and you'll have premature failure.

#### New Asphalt: Minimum 30 Days Cure

**The Rule**: Fresh asphalt should cure for a minimum of 30 days before applying pavement markings (some specs require 60-90 days).

**Why the Wait**:
- Fresh asphalt contains oils and volatiles that need to evaporate
- The binder film on the surface continues curing and hardening
- Early marking application can result in poor adhesion and premature fading
- Some marking materials (particularly thermoplastic) won't bond properly to uncured asphalt

**Testing for Readiness**:
- Water beading test: Spray water on the pavement. If it beads up, the surface is still too oily. When water absorbs or sheets off, it's closer to ready.
- Visual inspection: Fresh asphalt is deep black and oily-looking. Cured asphalt is lighter gray-black and drier in appearance.
- Surface tack: Touch the pavement. If it feels tacky or sticky, it's not ready.

**Temperature and Weather Effects**:
- Hot weather accelerates curing
- Cool or humid weather slows curing
- Fall paving may require longer cure time before spring striping

**Customer Communication**: "Your pavement needs to cure for at least 30 days before we can stripe it. This ensures the markings will adhere properly and last. We'll schedule striping for [date 30+ days out]."

**Managing Expectations**: For projects with immediate occupancy needs, explain the cure requirement upfront during bidding. Some customers don't know about this and will be frustrated if you surprise them after paving.

**Alternative**: If the customer absolutely must have temporary markings (for a grand opening, for example), traffic paint can sometimes be applied sooner (check with paint manufacturer), with the understanding that it may not last as long and will need to be redone. Document this clearly.

#### Surface Preparation

Proper surface prep is critical for marking adhesion and longevity—regardless of the marking material.

**Cleaning Requirements**:
- **Remove dirt, dust, and debris**: Sweep or blow off the surface
- **Remove oil and grease**: Use degreaser on any contaminated areas
- **Remove existing failed markings**: Old, loose paint or markings can interfere with adhesion of new materials
- **Dry the surface**: No standing water, no wet areas

**Why Prep Matters**: Marking materials bond to the asphalt surface. Any contamination—oil, dirt, water, loose material—creates a weak layer that leads to premature failure.

**Older Asphalt Considerations**:
- Aged asphalt may need light scarification or shot blasting to remove oxidized surface material
- Seal-coated surfaces should be allowed to cure (typically 48 hours minimum) before marking
- Recently crack-sealed areas may need cleaning to remove excess filler material

**Thermoplastic Specific**: For thermoplastic applications, the surface should be primed with a compatible primer to enhance adhesion, especially on older or seal-coated pavements.

**Pro Tip**: If you're coordinating striping with a subcontractor, make sure they understand surface condition. A freshly seal-coated parking lot might look great but won't take markings for a couple days.

#### Weather Conditions for Application

Pavement marking isn't just about the pavement—weather conditions during application directly affect quality and durability.

**Temperature Requirements**:
- **Paint**: Pavement temperature should be at least 40-50°F and rising
- **Thermoplastic**: Pavement temperature typically 50°F minimum (check manufacturer specs)
- **Tape and Epoxy**: Can be applied at lower temperatures (check product data)
- **Air temperature**: Should be at least 40°F and not expected to drop below freezing within 24 hours

**Moisture Conditions**:
- Pavement must be completely dry
- No rain, fog, or high humidity during application
- No rain expected for at least 2-4 hours after application (longer for paint)
- Morning dew must be completely evaporated

**Wind Conditions**:
- Excessive wind can affect spray application (overspray, uneven coverage)
- Dust and debris can blow onto fresh markings
- Shield critical areas if necessary

**Why Weather Matters**: 
- Cold pavement prevents proper curing and adhesion
- Moisture causes poor bond and adhesion failure
- Rain on fresh markings can wash away material or cause streaking

**Seasonal Considerations**:
- Late fall striping may not cure properly before winter
- Spring striping should wait until consistent temperatures above 50°F
- Summer is ideal for most marking materials
- Plan striping schedules accordingly and communicate timing to customers

**Customer Communication**: "We can't control the weather, but we can control when we stripe. We'll wait for good conditions to ensure the markings last. It's better to wait a few days than to have premature failure."

## 9.3 Roadway Markings

While most of your work may be parking lots and driveways, understanding roadway marking standards is essential if you're working on subdivision streets, private roads, or any project that connects to public roadways. The Manual on Uniform Traffic Control Devices (MUTCD) governs roadway markings nationwide, and local jurisdictions have specific requirements.

### Longitudinal Markings

Longitudinal markings run parallel to the direction of travel—lane lines, centerlines, and edge lines. They're the most common roadway markings you'll encounter.

#### Centerlines

**Purpose**: Separate opposing traffic flows on two-way roads.

**Standard Marking**:
- **Color**: Yellow (always yellow for opposing traffic)
- **Width**: 4-inch (standard), 5-6 inch (on high-speed roads)
- **Pattern**: Can be solid, broken, or double (indicates passing rules)

**Types of Centerlines**:
- **Single broken yellow**: Passing allowed for both directions (when safe)
- **Single solid yellow**: Passing discouraged, but not prohibited
- **Double solid yellow**: Passing prohibited for both directions (no-passing zone)
- **Combination solid/broken**: Passing allowed from the broken side only

**When Required**: On all two-way roadways, though may be omitted on very low-volume roads or private drives (check local standards).

**Subdivision Streets**: Most subdivision streets require centerline markings if they're two-way and more than 20 feet wide. Check your local code—some jurisdictions have specific requirements for residential streets.

#### Lane Lines

**Purpose**: Separate lanes of traffic moving in the same direction.

**Standard Marking**:
- **Color**: White (always white for same-direction traffic)
- **Width**: 4-inch (standard), 6-inch (on high-speed roads or for emphasis)
- **Pattern**: Typically broken (skip pattern), solid lines for restricted zones or edges

**Skip Pattern**:
- **Standard skip pattern**: 10 feet line, 30 feet gap (most common for speeds under 45 mph)
- **Highway skip pattern**: 10-12 feet line, 30-40 feet gap (for speeds over 45 mph)
- These ratios aren't arbitrary—they're based on driver perception and reaction time

**Solid Lane Lines**: Used where lane changes are discouraged or prohibited, such as:
- Approaching intersections
- In tunnels or narrow bridges
- Where sight distance is limited

**Multiple Lane Roads**: Each lane boundary needs marking. A four-lane road (two lanes each direction) needs a centerline and two lane lines.

#### Edge Lines

**Purpose**: Define the outside edge of the roadway, improving visibility and safety, especially at night or in poor weather.

**Standard Marking**:
- **Color**: White (right edge, direction of travel)
- **Width**: 4-inch (standard), 6-inch (on high-speed roads or where snowplows operate)
- **Pattern**: Solid line (continuous, not broken)

**When Required**: On all roads with average daily traffic (ADT) above a certain threshold (varies by jurisdiction), or on roads with speeds over 35-40 mph.

**Benefits**:
- Helps drivers maintain lane position at night
- Improves safety in fog, rain, or snow
- Provides clear boundary, reducing run-off-road crashes
- Assists snowplow operators in winter

**Urban vs. Rural**: Edge lines are more common on rural and suburban roads. Urban streets with curbs and gutters may not require edge lines, as the curb defines the edge.

#### Turn Lanes

**Purpose**: Designate lanes restricted to turning movements (left-turn or right-turn only).

**Marking Requirements**:
- **Lane line**: Solid white line separating turn lane from through lanes (usually 4-6 inches wide)
- **Turn arrows**: Pavement arrows indicating direction (left, right, or both)
- **Word marking**: "ONLY" often accompanies turn arrows

**Left-Turn Lanes**: May have yellow markings on the left side (separating from opposing traffic).

**Typical Configuration**: Solid white line on the right side of the turn lane, yellow marking on the left side (if centerline is present), arrow markings within the lane.

### Transverse Markings

Transverse markings run perpendicular to traffic flow—stop bars, crosswalks, yield lines, and legends. These markings often experience the heaviest wear because traffic rolls directly over them.

#### Stop Bars

**Purpose**: Indicate where vehicles must stop at intersections, stop signs, or traffic signals.

**Standard Marking**:
- **Color**: White
- **Width**: 12-24 inches wide (24 inches preferred for visibility)
- **Location**: 4 feet before the nearest crosswalk line, or if no crosswalk, 4 feet before the intersecting roadway edge
- **Extend across all approach lanes**: Stop bar should span all lanes that must stop

**Visibility is Critical**: Stop bars are high-wear locations. Use durable materials (thermoplastic, epoxy, or tape) for longer service life, or plan for frequent restriping with paint.

**Pro Tip**: A wide, bright stop bar improves safety and compliance. Skimping on stop bars is a false economy.

#### Crosswalks

**Purpose**: Designate pedestrian crossing areas and alert drivers to expect pedestrians.

**Standard Marking**:
- **Color**: White
- **Width**: 6-10 feet (in the direction of pedestrian travel)
- **Pattern**: Two parallel lines (transverse to traffic), or ladder/zebra pattern (alternating solid bars), or solid rectangular area

**Line Width**: The parallel lines defining the crosswalk are typically 12-24 inches wide.

**High-Visibility Crosswalks**: Ladder (zebra) pattern with solid bars perpendicular to traffic provides enhanced visibility and pedestrian safety. Often used at schools, hospitals, or high-pedestrian areas.

**Location**: Crosswalks are placed at intersections (all corners) or mid-block where pedestrian crossing is permitted and necessary.

**ADA Considerations**: Crosswalks must connect to accessible ramps at each end. Tactile warning surfaces (detectable warnings) are required at the transition from sidewalk to street.

**Durability Matters**: Crosswalks get heavy traffic and wear quickly. Thermoplastic or preformed tape is recommended for longevity. Paint crosswalks may need annual replacement.

#### Yield Lines

**Purpose**: Indicate where vehicles must yield to other traffic or pedestrians.

**Standard Marking**:
- **Color**: White
- **Pattern**: Series of solid white triangles pointing toward approaching traffic (creating a serrated line across the lane)
- **Width**: Triangles are typically 12-18 inches per side
- **Location**: 4 feet before the nearest crosswalk or conflict point

**When Used**: At yield signs, roundabouts, and locations where vehicles must yield to pedestrians or other vehicles.

**Less Common**: Yield lines are less common than stop bars in many areas, but increasingly used at roundabouts and modern intersection designs.

#### Speed Legends and Pavement Messages

**Purpose**: Communicate speed limits, warnings, or instructions to drivers.

**Standard Marking**:
- **Color**: White
- **Size**: Letters and numbers sized for readability at approach speed (typically 6-8 feet tall for roadways, smaller for parking lots)
- **Message**: "SPEED LIMIT 25," "SLOW," "STOP AHEAD," "SCHOOL," "PED XING," etc.

**Placement**: Messages are read in sequence by approaching traffic. Place text so the first word appears first in the direction of travel.

**Durability**: Because they're complex shapes, legends wear faster than simple lines. Use thermoplastic or preformed tape for longer service life.

**MUTCD Compliance**: Standard word messages and symbol shapes are specified in the MUTCD. Don't improvise creative messages that aren't in the standard—they may not be recognized or legally enforceable.

**Arrows**: Turn arrows, straight arrows, and combination arrows (straight/turn) guide drivers into correct lanes. Essential at intersections with multiple lanes.

### Reflectivity Requirements

Markings that aren't visible at night are worse than useless—they create a false sense of security. Reflectivity standards ensure markings remain visible in all conditions.

#### Nighttime Visibility Standards

**Why Reflectivity Matters**:
- Most serious traffic accidents occur at night
- Pavement markings must be visible in vehicle headlights
- Rain and wet conditions dramatically reduce marking visibility
- Aging markings lose reflectivity before they wear away

**How Reflectivity Works**: Glass beads are added to marking materials. Vehicle headlights hit the beads, reflect back to the driver's eyes, making the marking appear bright. This is called "retroreflectivity."

**MUTCD Requirements**: The MUTCD established minimum retroreflectivity levels for pavement markings (measured in millicandelas per lux per square meter, or mcd/lux/m²). Agencies are required to maintain markings above these minimums.

**Minimum Retroreflectivity Levels** (MUTCD 2009 and later):
- **White markings**: 100 mcd/lux/m² (new markings often exceed 300)
- **Yellow markings**: 75 mcd/lux/m² (new markings often exceed 200)

**Why This Matters to You**: If you're subcontracting striping, specify retroreflective materials and application. If you're bidding public work, retroreflectivity requirements will be in the spec.

#### Retroreflectivity Measurements

**How It's Measured**: Retroreflectivity is measured using a handheld retroreflectometer, which simulates headlight/eye geometry and measures the intensity of light reflected back.

**When Measurements are Required**:
- Acceptance testing on new markings (verify material meets spec)
- Periodic monitoring of existing markings to determine when replacement is needed
- Quality control during construction

**Who Does It**: Typically the agency, engineer, or quality control consultant measures retroreflectivity. Striping contractors may have their own equipment for internal QC.

**Practical Implication**: Low retroreflectivity means markings need replacement—even if they still look visible during the day. Faded reflectivity is a liability, not just an aesthetic issue.

#### Maintenance Schedules

Pavement markings don't last forever. Establishing realistic maintenance schedules helps customers budget and ensures safety.

**Paint Markings**:
- **Service Life**: 6-18 months depending on traffic volume and climate
- **Maintenance Cycle**: Annual restriping typical for moderate traffic, semi-annual for heavy traffic

**Thermoplastic Markings**:
- **Service Life**: 3-6 years in high traffic, 5-8 years in moderate traffic
- **Maintenance Cycle**: Inspect annually, replace when retroreflectivity falls below minimum or when physical wear is excessive

**Preformed Tape**:
- **Service Life**: 5-8 years (or longer in low traffic)
- **Maintenance Cycle**: Inspect annually, replace individual sections as needed

**Epoxy**:
- **Service Life**: 2-4 years depending on traffic and application thickness
- **Maintenance Cycle**: Inspect annually, restripe as needed

**Factors Affecting Service Life**:
- **Traffic volume**: Heavy traffic = faster wear
- **Snowplows**: Plow blades scrape markings away in winter climates
- **Climate**: UV exposure, temperature extremes, and moisture accelerate degradation
- **Material quality**: Higher-quality materials last longer

**Retroreflectivity Degradation**: Markings often lose retroreflectivity before they physically wear away. A marking may look fine in daylight but be invisible at night.

**Customer Communication**: "Based on the material we're using and your traffic levels, you should budget for restriping in [X] years. We can set up a maintenance schedule to keep your lot looking professional and safe."

**Business Opportunity**: Offering ongoing maintenance contracts for striping ensures regular work and recurring revenue. Customers appreciate predictable costs and professional upkeep.

## Closing Thoughts on Chapter 9

Striping, marking, and accessibility compliance might seem like the finishing touch—but they're far more than cosmetic. They're about safety, legal compliance, customer satisfaction, and professional reputation. A perfectly paved parking lot with sloppy striping is like a custom suit with wrinkled pants—it ruins the impression.

Here's what you should take away from this chapter:

**Know the Standards**: Understanding marking colors, widths, and applications prevents mistakes and builds credibility. When you can explain why accessible parking goes at the front entrance or why fire lanes need to be 24 feet wide, you're providing value beyond just moving asphalt.

**Timing Matters**: New asphalt needs 30+ days to cure before striping. Factor this into your schedule and communicate it clearly to customers. Rushing this step leads to failures and callbacks.

**ADA Compliance is Non-Negotiable**: Accessible parking requirements are federal law. Missing or incorrect accessible parking creates liability for your customer and reflects poorly on you. Get it right—dimensions, slopes, markings, signage, everything.

**Material Selection Affects Life-Cycle Cost**: Paint is cheap upfront but requires frequent replacement. Thermoplastic, epoxy, and tape cost more initially but last years longer. Help your customers understand the value proposition.

**Coordinate the Work**: Whether you're doing the striping yourself or subcontracting it, ensure proper coordination. Surface prep, cure time, weather conditions, material specifications—all these details matter for a quality job.

**Reflectivity Equals Safety**: Markings must be visible at night. Specify reflective materials and understand retroreflectivity requirements. This isn't optional—it's about preventing accidents.

**Offer Maintenance**: Striping wears out. Offering regular maintenance keeps your customers' properties looking good, ensures continued safety, and creates recurring revenue for your business.

The contractors who understand striping requirements, who coordinate the work seamlessly, who ensure ADA compliance, and who educate their customers on material options and maintenance needs—these are the contractors who deliver complete, professional projects. This leads to satisfied customers, positive referrals, and repeat business.

Striping might be the last thing you do on a project, but it's often the first thing people notice. Make it count.
