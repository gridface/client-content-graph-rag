# CHAPTER 7: TROUBLESHOOTING AND COMMON ISSUES

Welcome to what might be the most valuable chapter in this book for your day-to-day business. If you've been paving for any length of time, you know that problems happen—pavement cracks, ruts form, edges crumble, and customers call. The difference between contractors who thrive and those who struggle often comes down to one thing: the ability to identify problems early, understand what caused them, and fix them correctly the first time.

This chapter isn't just about repairing distressed pavement—it's about protecting your reputation and your profit margin. When you can walk a property owner through what went wrong, why it happened, and how to fix it properly, you're not making excuses—you're demonstrating expertise. When you can spot warning signs before minor issues become expensive failures, you save money and build customer loyalty. And when you understand the root causes of common distresses, you can avoid them on your next project.

Let's be clear: every contractor encounters pavement problems. The professionals know how to read them, diagnose them, and solve them. That's what we're covering here.

## 7.1 Cracking Distresses

Cracking is the most common type of asphalt pavement distress you'll encounter. Understanding the different types of cracks—and what causes them—is critical because the repair strategy depends entirely on correct diagnosis. Crack the wrong thing with crack sealant, and you've wasted your customer's money. Miss a structural issue underlying surface cracks, and you'll be back doing warranty work.

### 7.1.1 Alligator (Fatigue) Cracking

**What It Looks Like**

Alligator cracking gets its name from its appearance: interconnected cracks forming small blocks that look like alligator skin or chicken wire. These cracks start small—maybe just a few hairline cracks in the wheel path—but they propagate and interconnect over time, creating a network of polygonal pieces. In advanced stages, the surface can start breaking up and pumping water or mud when trucks drive over it.

**Why It Happens**

Alligator cracking is a **structural failure**. This is the pavement telling you it's overloaded—it can't handle the traffic it's seeing. Here's what's going on underneath:

**Repeated Traffic Loading (Fatigue)**: Every time a vehicle passes over pavement, the pavement flexes slightly—it bends under the load and springs back. Do this millions of times, and even a well-designed pavement will eventually fatigue, just like bending a paperclip back and forth until it breaks. The pavement wasn't necessarily poorly constructed—it may have simply reached the end of its structural capacity.

**Inadequate Pavement Thickness**: If the pavement structure is too thin for the traffic loads it's carrying, it will flex excessively with each load, accelerating fatigue. This is why proper thickness design matters—a 3-inch parking lot might be fine for cars, but it won't last under daily garbage truck traffic.

**Weak Subgrade or Base**: Even if the asphalt itself is thick enough, if it's sitting on a weak foundation—poor compaction, unsuitable soils, inadequate base material—the entire pavement structure deflects excessively. This transmits high tensile stresses to the bottom of the asphalt layer, where fatigue cracks initiate.

**Poor Drainage**: Water infiltration into the pavement structure, whether from the surface or through the edges, weakens the base and subgrade. When moisture gets into granular materials, it reduces their load-bearing capacity. Traffic on a saturated base pumps water and fine particles (this is called "pumping"), which creates voids and accelerates structural failure.

**Here's what you need to understand**: Alligator cracking **starts at the bottom of the asphalt layer** where tensile stresses are highest, then works its way to the surface. By the time you see it on top, the damage is already significant.

**Why This Matters for Your Business**

When a customer shows you alligator cracking and asks for a quote, you have a critical decision to make. If you just overlay it or patch the surface without addressing the structural problem, you're setting yourself up for a callback and an unhappy customer. This is where understanding the failure mode protects you.

Alligator cracking always occurs in **load-bearing areas**—wheel paths, turning areas, loading zones. If you're seeing it in low-traffic areas or non-wheel path locations, you need to ask more questions because something unusual is going on.

**How to Fix It**

The repair strategy depends entirely on severity:

**Early Stage (Low Severity)**: 
- Isolated areas with tight, interconnected cracks but no significant breakup
- **Repair**: Crack seal to prevent water infiltration, then place a seal coat or thin overlay (1 to 1.5 inches)
- **Timeline**: Do this now, before it gets worse
- **Cost Advantage**: This is the most economical repair stage, so identifying problems early saves your customer money

**Moderate Stage (Medium Severity)**:
- More extensive cracking with some spalling (edge raveling) at the cracks
- Slight to moderate breakup beginning
- **Repair**: Mill out the affected area to sound pavement (usually 1.5 to 2 inches minimum), then repave with proper compaction
- **Why**: You need to remove the damaged material before overlaying
- **Customer Explanation**: "The pavement structure has weakened in this area. We need to remove the damaged asphalt and replace it with a structural patch to restore the load-bearing capacity."

**Advanced Stage (High Severity)**:
- Significant breakup with loose pieces
- Pumping when loaded (water or mud coming through cracks)
- Often showing depressions or settling
- **Repair**: Full-depth repair to the base or subgrade level
  - Remove all failed pavement
  - Correct any base or subgrade issues (often requires recompaction or subgrade stabilization)
  - Rebuild the pavement structure properly from the bottom up
  - Cannot be fixed with overlays—you're throwing good money after bad if you try
- **Why**: The failure goes all the way through the pavement structure; surface repairs won't last

**Critical Point for Estimating**: When you're bidding a project with alligator cracking, you need to estimate the depth of repair accurately. If spec calls for "remove and replace to sound pavement," you need to account for the possibility of base repair. Include language in your proposal about additional costs if unexpected subgrade problems are encountered. This protects you from scope creep and gives you honest conversations with the customer if conditions are worse than expected.

### 7.1.2 Block Cracking

**What It Looks Like**

Block cracking forms large rectangular blocks, typically 1 foot by 1 foot or larger. The cracks are interconnected and relatively uniform, running roughly parallel and perpendicular to create a blocky pattern. Unlike alligator cracking, block cracks aren't limited to wheel paths—they can appear anywhere on the pavement surface.

**Why It Happens**

Block cracking is fundamentally different from alligator cracking. It's **not load-related**—it's a volumetric problem caused by the asphalt binder shrinking and expanding:

**Thermal Cycling**: Daily and seasonal temperature swings cause asphalt to expand and contract. Over many years, this cycling can lead to stress concentrations and shrinkage cracks that form block patterns.

**Aging and Oxidation**: As asphalt ages, the lighter oils in the binder evaporate or oxidize, leaving behind stiffer, more brittle asphaltenes. This aged asphalt can't accommodate thermal movement as well, so it cracks. You'll often see block cracking in areas with thin pavement that ages faster, or in mixes that were originally marginal in binder content.

**Low Penetration Asphalt**: If the mix was designed with a binder that's too stiff for the climate (think PG 76-22 in Minnesota), it won't have enough flexibility for cold weather, leading to premature cracking.

**Mix Placed Too Dry**: If the mix temperature is low during placement, the binder doesn't coat the aggregates thoroughly and the mix is difficult to compact. This results in a mix that's effectively lower in binder content than designed, making it more prone to shrinkage and cracking.

**Here's the key difference**: Block cracking reflects an asphalt mixture problem or aging issue, not a structural inadequacy. The pavement structure underneath is usually fine.

**Why This Matters for Your Business**

When you see block cracking, your diagnosis tells the customer a different story than alligator cracking: "This pavement is getting old and brittle. The surface needs rejuvenation or replacement, but the structure underneath is likely still sound." This is important because the repair is usually less extensive—and less expensive—than for structural failures.

Block cracking is your early warning system for pavement aging. If you're maintaining a customer's property and you start seeing block cracking develop, it's time to have a conversation about preventive maintenance before it progresses to raveling and potholes.

**How to Fix It**

**Early Stage**:
- Tight cracks, minimal spalling, surface still intact
- **Repair**: Crack seal all the cracks, then place a seal coat or rejuvenating seal
- **Why**: Crack sealing prevents water infiltration. Seal coating provides a new layer of protection and some binder rejuvenation
- **Customer Value**: This extends pavement life 3-5 years for minimal cost—typically $0.25 to $0.50 per square foot

**Moderate Stage**:
- Wider cracks with some spalling
- Beginning to show raveling at crack edges
- **Repair**: Mill and overlay with 1.5 to 2 inches of new asphalt
- **Why**: The surface asphalt is too brittle to maintain. Milling removes the damaged surface and provides a clean bond surface. The overlay provides a new wearing course with fresh binder
- **Customer Explanation**: "The top layer of asphalt has aged beyond the point where sealing will help. We need to remove it and replace it with fresh pavement."

**Advanced/Severe Stage**:
- Extensive interconnected cracks
- Significant spalling and raveling
- Some block pieces may be loose
- **Repair**: Mill deeper (2 to 3 inches) and overlay, or in severe cases, full reconstruction
- **Consideration**: At this stage, evaluate the entire pavement condition. If one area has aged this severely, other areas may be close behind

**Preventive Maintenance Note**: Block cracking is preventable. A properly maintained pavement with seal coating every 3-5 years and crack sealing as needed may never develop block cracking. This is a key selling point for maintenance contracts—regular maintenance is far cheaper than rehabilitation.

### 7.1.3 Edge Cracking

**What It Looks Like**

Edge cracking appears as cracks running parallel to the pavement edge, typically within 1 to 2 feet of the edge. The cracks may be continuous or segmented. In advanced cases, you'll see the pavement edge breaking away or settling lower than the main pavement.

**Why It Happens**

Edge cracking is almost always a **support problem**:

**Poor Drainage**: Water running off the pavement onto an unprotected edge, or water ponding along the edge, saturates the base and subgrade along the pavement edge. This creates a weak zone that can't support edge loads properly.

**Lack of Lateral Support**: Pavement edges need lateral confinement—either from curbing, edge restraints, or a properly compacted shoulder. Without it, the edge doesn't have the same structural capacity as the interior pavement. Traffic near the edge creates higher stresses because there's no lateral confinement.

**Vegetation Encroachment**: Grass, weeds, or tree roots growing along the pavement edge trap moisture and can actually push against or undermine the edge. I've seen tree roots lift and break pavement edges repeatedly.

**Settlement of Underlying Layers**: If the base or subgrade along the edge wasn't properly compacted during construction—or if it wasn't extended far enough beyond the pavement edge—differential settlement can occur, pulling the edge down and cracking it.

**Why This Matters for Your Business**

Edge cracking is often a sign of poor original construction or inadequate maintenance. This matters when you're bidding on repairs:

1. **Scope definition**: A simple edge crack repair that doesn't address the underlying support problem will fail. Your proposal needs to include fixing the drainage, stabilizing the base, and possibly adding edge support.

2. **Maintenance opportunity**: If you're seeing edge cracking on a property you maintain, it's likely visible to the property owner too. Proactively addressing it shows you're watching out for their investment.

3. **Preventive measures**: When you're constructing new pavement, proper edge detail—extending the base beyond the pavement edge, providing proper drainage, installing curb or edge restraint—prevents this problem. This is a quality differentiator you can explain to customers.

**How to Fix It**

**Early/Moderate Stage**:
- Crack is visible but edge hasn't broken away significantly
- **Repair Process**:
  1. Remove vegetation from the edge and at least 2 feet back from the pavement
  2. Correct any drainage problems (grade away from edge, add swales, clear ditches)
  3. Crack seal or rout and seal the cracks
  4. Monitor for progression
- **Why**: If you stop the water infiltration and vegetation growth, minor edge cracks can be stabilized
- **Cost**: Relatively inexpensive—mostly labor and crack sealant

**Advanced Stage**:
- Edge is breaking away or has broken away
- Significant spalling and deterioration
- Settlement visible along edge
- **Repair Process**:
  1. Saw cut a straight line 1 to 2 feet inside the damaged edge
  2. Remove all failed material back to the saw cut
  3. Excavate and recompact the base/subgrade (usually 6 to 12 inches beyond the new pavement edge)
  4. Apply tack coat to the vertical saw cut face
  5. Place new asphalt in lifts, compacting each thoroughly
  6. Ensure the new edge is supported (extend base, add edge support, or feather gradually to match existing grade)
- **Why**: You need to create a new, structurally sound edge. Half-measures don't work
- **Customer Explanation**: "The edge has lost support and needs to be rebuilt properly. We'll remove the failed section, stabilize the foundation, and rebuild it so this doesn't happen again."

**Critical Construction Detail**: When placing the patch, many contractors make the mistake of not extending their base wide enough or not compacting it properly. The base should extend at least 6 inches beyond the pavement edge, and it needs proper compaction. If you don't do this, you're just creating tomorrow's edge crack.

**Preventive Measures for New Construction**:
- Extend base course at least 6 inches beyond pavement edge
- Provide positive drainage away from pavement edges
- Consider curb, edge banding, or shoulders for high-traffic areas
- Keep vegetation at least 2 feet from pavement edge
- On residential driveways, consider widening the apron or adding edge support where cars track off

### 7.1.4 Longitudinal and Transverse Cracking

**What It Looks Like**

These are linear cracks running either parallel to the pavement centerline (longitudinal) or perpendicular to it (transverse). Unlike the interconnected patterns of alligator or block cracking, these are typically single, continuous cracks. They can be tight hairline cracks or wider cracks with spalling, depending on age and severity.

**Why It Happens**

The causes vary depending on the crack location and orientation:

**Thermal Stress (Low-Temperature Cracking)**:
- This is the classic reason for transverse cracks in cold climates
- When pavement gets extremely cold, the asphalt contracts (shrinks)
- If the pavement is restrained (friction with the base, edges are confined), tensile stresses build up
- Eventually the stress exceeds the tensile strength of the aged, cold, stiff asphalt and it cracks—usually perpendicular to the direction of greatest stress
- **Pattern**: Transverse cracks roughly perpendicular to centerline, often spaced fairly regularly (every 20 to 100 feet)
- **Timing**: Typically appears after several years of service as binder ages and becomes less flexible

**Paving Joint Failures**:
- Longitudinal cracks that follow the paving lane joint are usually construction-related
- Caused by: poor joint construction (didn't overlap properly, didn't get tack coat on the vertical face, joint wasn't hot enough when placed), different age mixes (overlaying old pavement with new, but not milling the joint area), or differential compaction across the joint
- **Pattern**: Crack runs along the lane joint (parallel to centerline), often with some spalling

**Reflection Cracking from Underlying Layers**:
- If there's a crack or joint in the base layer or in an old asphalt layer below, stresses concentrate at that location
- Thermal and traffic-induced movements in the underlying layer propagate through the new asphalt surface
- **Pattern**: Can be either longitudinal or transverse, depending on what's underneath. Common where new asphalt is placed over existing cracked pavement without milling

**Inadequate Joint Construction**:
- Longitudinal construction joints that weren't properly made (cold joint, no tack coat, poor compaction)
- These show up early—sometimes within the first year—because they were never bonded properly

**Why This Matters for Your Business**

Understanding the difference between these crack causes is critical for two reasons:

1. **Warranty and Liability**: If longitudinal cracks along your paving joints show up shortly after construction, that's likely a construction defect. You need to go back and correct it. If transverse cracks show up after 5+ years in a cold climate, that's often normal aging and thermal stress—not a construction problem.

2. **Repair Recommendations**: A thermal crack needs crack sealing and possibly an overlay with a stress-absorbing interlayer. A joint failure needs to be removed and reconstructed with proper joint construction. A one-size-fits-all repair won't work.

**How to Fix It**

**Crack Sealing (for stable, non-working cracks)**:
- Clean the crack thoroughly (router or compressed air)
- Apply hot-pour crack sealant using proper equipment
- Overfill slightly and squeegee to shape
- **When to Use**: For thermal cracks, longitudinal cracks that aren't actively failing, cracks less than 3/8 inch wide
- **Limitation**: This is a maintenance treatment—it stops water infiltration but doesn't restore structural integrity

**Rout and Seal (for wider or more active cracks)**:
- Use a crack routing machine to create a reservoir (typically 3/4 to 1 inch wide, 1 to 1 inch deep)
- Clean thoroughly
- Apply backer rod if needed
- Fill with hot-pour sealant
- **When to Use**: Cracks wider than 1/2 inch, cracks that show movement, cracks with spalling at edges
- **Advantage**: Larger reservoir holds more sealant, provides better long-term performance

**Mill and Overlay with Crack Relief Interlayer (for numerous cracks or reflective cracking)**:
- Mill off the top 1.5 to 2 inches
- Apply a stress-absorbing membrane interlayer (paving fabric or specialized membrane)
- Overlay with new asphalt
- **When to Use**: Multiple cracks indicating overall pavement aging, overlays over concrete (to delay reflection cracking), thin pavement that's cracking due to age
- **Customer Benefit**: The interlayer absorbs stress and slows down the reflection of cracks through the new overlay, extending life by 3 to 5 years compared to overlay alone

**Remove and Replace (for failed joints or severe cracking)**:
- Saw cut around the affected area (typically 2 to 4 feet wide for a longitudinal joint failure)
- Remove full depth
- Verify base condition, repair if needed
- Tack coat thoroughly
- Replace with proper lift thickness and compaction
- **When to Use**: Longitudinal joint failures with significant spalling, localized areas where transverse cracks have led to significant deterioration

**Customer Communication**:
When discussing cracks with customers, distinguish between maintenance-level repairs and structural repairs:
- "These are thermal cracks from our cold winters. They're common in aged pavement. Crack sealing prevents water infiltration and extends life by 3 to 5 years before you need an overlay."
- vs.
- "This longitudinal crack along the paving joint shows the joint wasn't constructed properly. We need to remove and replace this section to ensure proper bonding and longevity."

This distinction helps customers understand what they're paying for and sets proper expectations.

### 7.1.5 Joint Reflection Cracking

**What It Looks Like**

If you're overlaying asphalt over an existing concrete pavement (composite pavement), you'll likely see cracks appear in the asphalt overlay directly above the joints and cracks in the concrete below. These cracks mirror the pattern of the underlying concrete pavement—typically forming a rectangular grid pattern matching the concrete slab joints (often 15 to 20 feet on center).

**Why It Happens**

Concrete and asphalt behave very differently:

**Thermal Expansion and Contraction**:
- Concrete slabs expand and contract with temperature
- The joints between slabs are designed to accommodate this movement
- When asphalt is placed over concrete, the concrete joints continue to move underneath
- This movement creates stress concentrations in the asphalt directly above the joint
- Eventually, the asphalt cracks directly over the joint

**Traffic Loading**:
- Concrete slabs can rock slightly at joints when traffic passes over them, especially if there's pumping (loss of base support)
- This rocking motion flexes the asphalt overlay, creating bending stress
- Repeated loading at these stress points causes fatigue cracking

**Vertical Movement (Faulting)**:
- If adjacent concrete slabs are at different elevations (faulting), traffic experiences a bump at each joint
- This impact loads the overlay, concentrating stress at the joint
- Reflection cracking occurs faster

**The Bottom Line**: It's nearly impossible to prevent reflection cracking permanently when overlaying concrete. The question isn't if it will happen, but when—and how you can delay it.

**Why This Matters for Your Business**

When bidding concrete overlay projects, you need to set realistic expectations:

1. **Educate the Customer**: Reflection cracking is normal and expected. Your proposal should mention it: "Reflection cracking from the underlying concrete joints typically begins appearing within 1 to 3 years. This is a characteristic of concrete overlay projects, not a defect."

2. **Offer Mitigation Options**: Explain the cost-benefit of crack relief systems upfront. Customers need to decide if the added cost is worth delaying the inevitable.

3. **Warranty Considerations**: Be very clear about what you will and won't warranty on concrete overlays. Reflection cracking shouldn't be a warranty issue if you've explained it properly.

**How to Fix It**

**Initial Overlay Strategy (Prevention):**

While you can't prevent reflection cracking permanently, you can delay it significantly:

**Crack Relief Interlayer Systems**:
- **Stress-Absorbing Membrane Interlayer (SAMI)**: A layer of polymer-modified asphalt or rubber-modified binder applied between the concrete and the asphalt overlay. It acts as a stress-relief layer that can accommodate small movements
- **Paving Fabrics**: Nonwoven geotextile fabrics saturated with asphalt binder. These provide some reinforcement and stress relief
- **Crack Relief Layer**: A specialized asphalt mixture designed with higher binder content and sometimes rubber modification
- **Cost-Benefit**: These add $0.50 to $2.00 per square yard but can delay reflection cracking by 2 to 5 years. On a large project, that's significant value.

**Adequate Overlay Thickness**:
- Thicker overlays delay reflection cracking longer—the stresses have more thickness to dissipate through
- Minimum 2 inches, but 3 to 4 inches is better for longevity
- **Trade-off**: Thicker overlays cost more initially but last longer

**Concrete Repair Before Overlay**:
- Fix faulted or rocking slabs before overlaying
- Grind down significant surface irregularities
- Undersealing or slab stabilization for slabs with pumping issues
- **Why**: Reducing the amount of movement in the concrete reduces stress on the overlay

**Repair After Reflection Cracking Occurs:**

Once reflection cracks appear, your options are:

**Crack Sealing (short-term maintenance)**:
- Seal the cracks as they appear to prevent water infiltration
- This extends the life of the overlay but doesn't fix the underlying problem
- Expect to re-seal every 2 to 3 years as cracks widen and new ones form

**Remove and Replace the Overlay**:
- Mill off the overlay
- Address any concrete pavement issues (spalled joints, faulting)
- Install a crack relief system (if not done originally)
- Place new overlay
- **When to Use**: When the overlay has extensive reflection cracking and raveling, typically after 10 to 15 years

**Full Reconstruction**:
- Remove the concrete pavement entirely
- Rebuild with full-depth asphalt or new concrete
- **When to Use**: When the concrete is severely deteriorated, or when the benefit of eliminating reflection cracking justifies the high cost

**Customer Communication**:
"Overlaying concrete gives you a smooth, quiet ride for less cost than full reconstruction, but reflection cracking is part of the trade-off. We can delay it with crack relief systems, but it will eventually appear. The alternative—full reconstruction—costs about 50% more but eliminates this issue. Here's what each option costs..."

This honest conversation builds trust and lets customers make informed decisions.

### 7.1.6 Slippage Cracking

**What It Looks Like**

Slippage cracking has a distinctive appearance: crescent-shaped or half-moon cracks that look like "stretch marks." The crescents point in the direction of the applied force—usually you'll see them in areas where traffic accelerates, decelerates, or turns. Unlike other cracks that are straight or polygonal, these have a curved, swept appearance.

**Why It Happens**

Slippage cracking is a **bond failure between pavement layers**. The top layer is literally slipping over the layer below:

**Poor Bond Between Asphalt Layers**:
- The most common cause
- When a new lift is placed over an existing surface without proper preparation, the layers don't bond
- Under traffic—especially in areas with high shear stress like curves, intersections, or turning lanes—the top layer slides over the bottom layer
- This creates the distinctive crescent-shaped cracks

**Lack of Tack Coat**:
- Tack coat (a thin layer of asphalt emulsion) is essential for bonding new asphalt to old asphalt or concrete
- If tack coat is skipped, insufficient, or improperly applied, there's no adhesion
- This is a construction defect—it should never happen

**Contamination Preventing Adhesion**:
- Dirt, dust, oil, grease, or water on the surface before overlay prevents tack coat from bonding
- Common in gas stations (fuel spills), industrial areas (grease), or areas not properly cleaned before paving
- **Prevention**: The surface must be clean and dry before tack coat application

**Hot Mix Placed on Wet Surface**:
- Even without contamination, placing asphalt on a wet surface prevents proper bonding
- The water flashes to steam, creating air pockets between layers
- Never pave on wet pavement

**Why This Matters for Your Business**

Slippage cracking is a **construction quality control issue**. If it shows up on your work, you need to fix it under warranty—it's not a design problem or a materials problem, it's a construction defect.

This matters for:

1. **Quality Control**: Your paving crew needs to understand the importance of proper tack coat application: surface must be clean and dry, tack coat must be applied uniformly at the correct rate, tack coat must break (turn from brown to black) before paving over it, and rolling pattern must promote bonding between lifts.

2. **Pre-Overlay Inspection**: When bidding overlay projects, check for oil stains, fuel spills, or other contamination. These areas need special cleaning or removal before overlay. Include this in your scope and price—don't absorb the cost later.

3. **Warranty Language**: Be specific about surface preparation requirements. If the owner wants to pave over an area you've identified as contaminated without proper cleaning, document it and exclude bond failure from your warranty.

**How to Fix It**

There's really only one proper repair for slippage cracking:

**Full-Depth Removal and Replacement**:
- Saw cut the affected area, extending well beyond the visible slippage cracks (typically 10 feet past the last crack)
- Remove asphalt down to the layer below the slippage plane
  - If slippage is between the surface course and binder course, remove the surface course
  - If slippage is between two lifts in the surface course, remove both
- Clean the exposed surface thoroughly
- Apply proper tack coat at the specified rate (typically 0.04 to 0.06 gallons per square yard of residual asphalt)
- Allow tack coat to break
- Place new asphalt in proper lift thickness
- Compact thoroughly, paying special attention to achieving density and bonding

**Why Full-Depth Removal**:
- There's no way to re-bond layers that have already slipped
- Surface-only repairs (crack sealing, seal coating) won't work because the underlying layers are still unbonded
- Trying to overlay over slippage cracking just moves the problem up one layer—the new overlay will slip over the unbonded layers below

**Cost Implication**:
This is an expensive repair—typically $15 to $30 per square yard depending on depth. This is why prevention through proper construction is so important.

**Prevention in New Work**:

Make tack coat application a non-negotiable quality control point:

1. **Surface Preparation**: Sweep or blow clean. No dust, debris, or loose material. If wet, wait until dry.

2. **Contamination Removal**: Remove oil stains by milling, shot blasting, or solvent cleaning. Fresh tack coat won't adhere to oil.

3. **Tack Coat Application**: 
   - Use proper application rate: typically 0.04 to 0.06 gal/sy for existing asphalt, 0.08 to 0.12 gal/sy for concrete or milled surfaces
   - Apply uniformly—no puddles, no missed spots
   - Wait for tack coat to break (emulsion turns from brown to black)
   - Don't track tack onto adjacent surfaces

4. **Placement**: 
   - Don't allow tracked equipment to churn or tear the tacked surface
   - Place mix while tack is still sticky
   - On hot days, if tack has dried, consider light re-tack

5. **Compaction**:
   - Initial breakdown rolling should knead the new lift into the tack coat
   - Proper density in both lifts promotes interlayer bonding

**Customer Communication**:
If you encounter slippage cracking on someone else's work and you're providing a repair bid: "This type of cracking indicates the pavement layers aren't bonded together properly. It's a construction-related issue. The only effective repair is to remove and replace the affected area with proper surface preparation and tack coat application. Temporary fixes won't work."

If it's your work under warranty: "We identified a bond issue in this area. We're going to remove and replace this section properly—clean the surface thoroughly, apply proper tack coat, and rebuild it. This is covered under warranty and we'll make it right."

Honesty and accountability build reputation.

## 7.2 Surface Deformation Distresses

Surface deformation distresses are permanent changes in the pavement shape. Unlike cracking, which involves actual breaks in the pavement, deformations are characterized by displacement—the pavement has moved, rut, or deformed under traffic or other stresses. These distresses are often load-related and can significantly impact ride quality, drainage, and safety.

### 7.2.1 Rutting

**What It Looks Like**

Rutting appears as longitudinal depressions in the wheel paths. Stand at one end of a parking lot or roadway and sight down the lanes—you'll see parallel grooves where the wheels track. In severe cases, ruts can be 1 inch deep or more. Water often ponds in ruts during rain, creating hydroplaning hazards and accelerating pavement deterioration.

**Why It Happens**

Rutting is caused by **permanent deformation** of one or more pavement layers. The material is literally being displaced by traffic loads:

**Consolidation or Lateral Movement of Materials**:
- Traffic loads compress the pavement layers (consolidation) or push material sideways out of the wheel path (lateral displacement)
- The pavement "flows" under load like a very stiff fluid
- This is often called "plastic deformation"

**Inadequate Compaction**:
- If the asphalt wasn't compacted to proper density during construction, continued traffic consolidates the mix
- **Rule of thumb**: Properly compacted HMA should reach 92% to 96% of laboratory density. Below 92%, you'll see rutting
- This is a construction defect—it shouldn't happen if proper compaction procedures were followed

**Weak Asphalt Mix**:
- Mix design problems that lead to rutting:
  - Too much asphalt binder (fat mix)
  - Rounded, smooth aggregates with poor internal friction
  - Insufficient aggregate contact (too much fine aggregate, not enough coarse)
  - Binder that's too soft for the climate and traffic (wrong PG grade)
- These mixes can't resist shear stress under heavy loads, especially at high temperatures

**High Temperatures and Heavy Loads**:
- Rutting is most severe in summer when pavement temperatures are high
- Hot asphalt is much softer and more susceptible to deformation
- Slow-moving or standing heavy loads (busses at stops, trucks at signals) generate the highest pavement temperatures and longest load durations
- The combination of high temperature, heavy loads, and slow speed is worst-case for rutting

**Subgrade or Base Inadequacy**:
- If rutting goes deep—if the entire pavement cross-section is depressed, not just surface grooves—the problem is probably in the base or subgrade
- Weak subgrade soils (high clay content, poor compaction) or inadequate base thickness can rut under traffic
- Poor drainage leading to saturated base materials reduces load-bearing capacity

**Here's a critical distinction**: Surface rutting (top 1 to 2 inches only) is usually an asphalt mix or compaction problem. Full-depth rutting (entire pavement section depressed) is usually a structural problem with base or subgrade.

**Why This Matters for Your Business**

Rutting is a common complaint on commercial properties, especially in high-traffic areas like drive-through lanes, entrance drives, and loading areas. Understanding the cause is essential for proposing the right repair:

1. **Liability**: If rutting appears within the first few years on work you did, it's likely a construction or mix design issue. This may be a warranty claim. Proper compaction records (including density testing) protect you.

2. **Diagnosis Tools**: Walk the site in the rain or after rain. Where is water ponding? In the wheel paths (surface rutting) or across the entire lane (full-depth rutting)? This tells you where the problem is.

3. **Customer Education**: Many customers don't understand why rutting happens. Explaining "Heavy truck traffic and high summer temperatures can cause the pavement to deform over time, especially if the pavement was originally undersized for this level of traffic" helps them understand it's not always a construction defect.

**How to Fix It**

The repair depends on the severity and depth of rutting:

**Minor Rutting (less than 1/2 inch depth)**:
- **Repair**: Mill and overlay
  - Mill out the rutted surface (typically 1.5 to 2 inches)
  - Place new overlay
- **Why This Works**: Removes the deformed material and provides a new, smooth surface
- **Cost**: Typically $2 to $4 per square foot
- **Consideration**: If the rutting was caused by a weak mix, make sure the overlay mix is properly designed for the traffic and climate

**Moderate Rutting (1/2 to 1 inch depth)**:
- **Repair**: Mill affected lanes and repave
  - Mill deep enough to remove all rutting (usually 2 to 3 inches minimum)
  - If rutting is only in wheel paths, consider milling and leveling course before final surface
  - Place new surface course with appropriate mix design
- **Customer Explanation**: "We need to mill down below the rut depth to remove all the deformed asphalt, then rebuild with a properly designed mix that can handle your traffic loads."

**Severe Rutting (greater than 1 inch depth) or Full-Depth Rutting**:
- **Diagnosis First**: Core through the pavement to determine if rutting is in the asphalt only or if base/subgrade is involved
- **Repair for Surface Rutting**:
  - Remove and replace the asphalt layers completely
  - Verify the mix design is appropriate (may need PMA or SMA for heavy traffic)
- **Repair for Full-Depth Rutting**:
  - Remove asphalt completely
  - Evaluate base and subgrade
  - Repair or replace weak base material
  - Improve drainage if moisture is a factor
  - Increase pavement thickness if original design was inadequate
  - Reconstruct with proper compaction at each layer
- **Cost**: Full reconstruction can be $8 to $15+ per square foot

**Drainage Considerations**:
- If moisture is contributing to rutting (saturated base or subgrade), you MUST address drainage as part of the repair
- Add underdrains, improve surface drainage, repair failing storm drains
- Failing to address drainage means you'll be back repairing ruts again in a few years

**Prevention in New Construction**:

This is where your quality control during construction pays off:

1. **Proper Mix Design**:
   - Use mixes designed for the expected traffic level
   - For heavy traffic, consider SMA, PMA, or high-binder-content mixes with proven rut resistance
   - Verify binder grade is appropriate for climate and traffic (bump up for slow-moving heavy loads)

2. **Compaction**:
   - Achieve proper density: 92% to 96% of lab density
   - Use nuclear density gauge or cores to verify
   - Document your compaction testing—it protects you if rutting occurs later
   - Pay extra attention to compaction in wheel paths

3. **Construction Practices**:
   - Don't allow heavy construction traffic on fresh asphalt before it cools
   - Avoid paving in excessively hot weather if possible (mix tends to be tender)
   - Use proper rolling pattern (breakdown, intermediate, finish)

4. **Structural Design**:
   - Make sure the pavement thickness is adequate for the expected traffic
   - Don't cut corners on base thickness—a strong base supports the asphalt
   - Ensure proper drainage to keep base and subgrade dry

**Customer Communication**:
"Rutting in the drive lane is caused by heavy trucks repeatedly driving the same path. Over time, especially in hot weather, this causes the asphalt to compact and displace. We can mill out the rutted areas and repave with a mix designed specifically for heavy-duty use. This will restore the surface and provide better long-term performance."

### 7.2.2 Corrugation and Shoving

**What It Looks Like**

Corrugation (sometimes called "washboarding") appears as ripples or waves across the pavement surface, perpendicular to the direction of traffic. The ripples are fairly regular, often spaced 1 to 3 feet apart. Shoving is similar but typically appears as a single wave or bulge rather than multiple ripples. Both are most common in areas where traffic starts, stops, accelerates, or turns.

**Why It Happens**

**Unstable Asphalt Layer**:
- The asphalt mix is too plastic—it can't resist the horizontal shear stresses from traffic
- The mix essentially "flows" under traffic, pushed forward (in front of braking areas) or pulled along (by acceleration forces)

**Poor Mix Design or Manufacturing**:
- Too much asphalt binder makes the mix tender and unstable
- Low aggregate friction (rounded aggregates, insufficient coarse aggregate)
- Mix placed too hot or not compacted properly when placed
- Contamination (dirt, fines) making the mix unstable

**Frequent Traffic Start/Stop Locations**:
- Occurs at intersections (especially on approaches to stop signs or signals)
- At toll booths
- At railroad crossings
- At sharp curves
- Anywhere vehicles are braking, accelerating, or turning sharply
- **Why**: These locations experience high horizontal shear forces that push the asphalt

**High Horizontal Shear Stresses**:
- Braking pushes pavement forward
- Acceleration pulls pavement along
- Turning applies lateral shear
- If the mix can't resist these forces, it deforms

**Why This Matters for Your Business**

Corrugation and shoving are red flags for mix problems. If you see this on your projects, investigate immediately:

1. **Quality Control Issue**: Check your mix design. Check your supplier's quality control. Was the mix manufactured correctly? Was it placed too hot?

2. **Site-Specific Problem**: These distresses often appear in specific, predictable locations. When bidding, look for evidence of shoving at intersections—it tells you the existing mix couldn't handle the stress, and you'll need a higher-performance mix for the repair.

3. **Warranty Exposure**: If corrugation shows up within the first year or two on a project you paved, this is likely a mix or construction issue. This is a warranty claim.

**How to Fix It**

**Repair Process**:
- **Remove the Unstable Material**: 
  - Mill or remove the corrugated/shoved area, plus at least 10 feet beyond
  - Remove full depth of the unstable layer (don't leave any of it—it's a weak layer)
- **Replace with Proper Mix Design**:
  - Use a stiffer, more rut-resistant mix
  - Consider SMA, PMA, or a mix with higher PG binder grade
  - Ensure the mix has good aggregate interlock (angular aggregates, appropriate gradation)
- **Proper Compaction**:
  - Achieve proper density
  - Don't over-roll (which can shove the mix)
  - Use proper rolling temperatures

**Why Full Removal is Necessary**:
- You can't just overlay corrugated pavement—the waves will telegraph through
- The underlying material is unstable, so adding more material on top won't help
- Leveling courses can help somewhat, but removing the unstable mix is the permanent solution

**Cost**: Similar to moderate rutting repair—$3 to $6 per square foot depending on depth and mix design.

**Prevention**:
- Use proven mix designs for high-stress areas (intersections, curves)
- Consider bumping up the PG grade in braking/acceleration zones
- Ensure proper compaction without over-rolling
- Don't use "economy" mixes in high-stress locations—spend a little more on the mix and save a lot on the repairs

**Customer Communication**:
"These ripples are caused by unstable asphalt that couldn't resist the braking and acceleration forces. We need to remove this material and replace it with a higher-performance mix designed specifically for intersection use. This will eliminate the problem."

### 7.2.3 Depressions (Birdbaths)

**What It Looks Like**

Depressions—often called "birdbaths" because they collect water like a birdbath—are localized low areas in the pavement. They're not widespread like rutting, but rather isolated spots, typically circular or irregular in shape, that are noticeably lower than the surrounding pavement. After rain, these are obvious because they're the spots with standing water.

**Why It Happens**

**Settlement of Base or Subgrade**:
- The most common cause
- If the base or subgrade wasn't properly compacted during construction, it consolidates under traffic over time
- The asphalt on top follows the settlement, creating a depression
- Soft spots in the subgrade (wet clay, organic materials, poor soils) settle more than surrounding areas

**Poor Initial Compaction**:
- If the base, subgrade, or asphalt layers weren't compacted to proper density during construction, continued traffic loads cause ongoing consolidation
- This is particularly common around utility trenches where backfill wasn't adequately compacted

**Inadequate Design**:
- If the pavement structure is inadequate for the loads it's experiencing, localized failure can occur
- Weak areas in the subgrade that weren't discovered during construction

**Why This Matters for Your Business**

Depressions are visible problems that customers complain about. They affect drainage, create unsafe conditions (hydroplaning, ice buildup), and signal underlying problems.

1. **Trenching and Utilities**: If you're paving over recently dug utility trenches, pay extra attention to compaction. Trench settlements are very common and create liability issues. Many contractors include language in proposals stating "settlement over utility trenches repaired by others is not warranted."

2. **Existing Depressions**: If you're overlaying existing pavement with depressions, you MUST address them before overlaying or they'll telegraph through. Include this work in your scope.

3. **Customer Complaints**: Customers see standing water and they call. Being proactive about identifying and addressing depressions during your walk-through shows attentiveness.

**How to Fix It**

**Temporary/Interim Repair**:
- **Surface Patching**: 
  - Level the depression with asphalt mix
  - Feather to match surrounding grade
  - Compact properly
- **Why This is Temporary**: If the settlement is due to continued consolidation of base or subgrade, the patch will settle too. You may need to patch multiple times.
- **When to Use**: As a temporary measure while waiting for further settlement to stabilize, or as a low-cost option if the customer understands it may settle again

**Permanent Repair**:
- **Remove and Replace with Subgrade Repair**:
  1. Mark the extent of the depression (usually visible even when dry)
  2. Saw cut and remove asphalt in the depressed area (typically extend cut 2 to 3 feet beyond visible depression)
  3. Excavate down to stable subgrade (often 12 to 24 inches or more if subgrade is weak)
  4. If subgrade is wet or weak:
     - Remove unsuitable material
     - Bring in suitable granular material
     - Compact in lifts (4 to 6 inch lifts, compacted to 95%+ Proctor density)
  5. Rebuild base to match surrounding elevation
  6. Compact base properly
  7. Tack coat
  8. Replace asphalt in lifts, compacting each
  9. Match surrounding surface elevation and grade

**Why Full-Depth Repair Works**:
- Addresses the underlying cause (weak subgrade or base)
- Prevents recurrence
- Restores proper drainage

**Cost**: $15 to $40+ per square yard depending on depth of excavation and subgrade conditions.

**Special Consideration for Utility Trenches**:
- If the depression is over a utility trench, coordinate with the utility to determine who is responsible for repair
- Document the settlement before your repair—photos, measurements, dates
- If you're repairing someone else's trench settlement, make sure your proposal clearly states the scope and doesn't include warranty for future settlement

**Prevention in New Construction**:

Proper compaction is the key:
- **Subgrade**: Proof roll after initial compaction. Any soft areas need remediation before paving.
- **Base**: Compact in lifts no thicker than 6 inches. Test for proper density.
- **Asphalt**: Compact to proper density (92% to 96%).
- **Utility Trenches**: If you're paving over trenches, require proof of proper trench backfill compaction. Consider adding extra base over trenches.

**Customer Communication**:
"This low spot is caused by settlement in the base or subgrade below the pavement. A surface patch is a temporary fix that may settle again. For a permanent repair, we need to remove the asphalt, repair the underlying layers, and rebuild the pavement properly. This costs more initially but prevents the problem from recurring."

Give customers the choice—temporary fix now with potential future repairs, or permanent fix once. Most will appreciate the honesty and choose the permanent solution.

## 7.3 Disintegration Distresses

Disintegration distresses involve the actual breakdown of the pavement material itself. Unlike deformation (where material moves) or cracking (where material separates), disintegration is the loss of material—either aggregate particles coming loose from the surface or complete breakdown of the pavement structure. These distresses often indicate aging, poor mix quality, or water infiltration.

### 7.3.1 Raveling (Weathering)

**What It Looks Like**

Raveling is the progressive loss of aggregate particles from the pavement surface. It starts with small surface rocks becoming loose and popping out under traffic. Over time, the surface gets rougher and more pockmarked as more aggregate is lost. In advanced stages, large areas of aggregate are missing, leaving a rough, deteriorated surface that's harsh on tires and can become a loose gravel hazard.

The deterioration works from the surface downward—you'll see aggregate gradually disappearing, leaving binder-rich areas that eventually wear away too, exposing more aggregate underneath.

**Why It Happens**

**Oxidation and Aging**:
- Over time, UV radiation, oxygen, and temperature cycling cause the asphalt binder to oxidize and harden
- As binder ages, it becomes brittle and loses its ability to hold aggregate particles
- The binder literally crumbles and wears away, releasing the aggregate it once held
- This is a natural aging process but can be accelerated by thin pavement (more exposure), high UV exposure, or marginal mix designs

**Poor Compaction**:
- If the mix wasn't properly compacted during construction, there's less contact between aggregate particles and less binder adhesion
- Poorly compacted areas ravel much faster than properly compacted areas
- **Tip**: If raveling appears in distinct patterns—like handwork areas, areas around utility structures, or edges—poor compaction is the likely culprit

**Low-Quality Mix (Insufficient Binder)**:
- If the mix was designed with too little binder, or if the mix was manufactured below specification, there isn't enough binder to hold the aggregate
- "Dry-looking" mixes—mixes that look brown or gray rather than black—are often low on binder
- This is a mix design or manufacturing problem

**Inadequate Curing** (for cold mixes and emulsion applications):
- Emulsion-based mixes and surface treatments need time for the water to evaporate before traffic
- If traffic is allowed too soon, the incompletely cured binder can be stripped away by tires, leading to raveling
- Always follow manufacturer's curing time recommendations

**Freeze-Thaw Damage**:
- Water infiltration into the surface, followed by freezing, can pop aggregate particles loose
- Moisture can also cause stripping (loss of binder-aggregate adhesion) which leads to raveling
- More common in cold climates with poor drainage

**Why This Matters for Your Business**

Raveling is a maintenance indicator. When customers call about "gravel coming up from the parking lot" or "rough pavement surface," they're describing raveling. This is your opportunity to provide preventive maintenance (early stage) or rehabilitation (advanced stage):

1. **Preventive Maintenance Opportunity**: Early-stage raveling can be arrested with seal coating. This is a relatively inexpensive service that extends pavement life. If you have maintenance customers, monitoring for raveling and recommending seal coating at the right time builds long-term relationships.

2. **Quality Control**: If raveling appears on relatively new pavement (less than 5 years old), investigate whether it's a compaction issue or a mix quality issue. This could be warranty-related.

3. **Surface Treatment Failure**: Raveling after chip seals, slurry seals, or other surface treatments often indicates improper application or poor curing. Make sure you're following manufacturer specifications and best practices.

**How to Fix It**

The repair strategy depends entirely on severity:

**Early Stage** (Just beginning—aggregate starting to loosen on surface):
- **Repair**: Seal coating
  - Apply a coal tar or asphalt emulsion seal coat
  - The seal coat provides a new protective layer that binds loose aggregate and protects the underlying binder from further oxidation
- **Cost**: Typically $0.15 to $0.30 per square foot
- **Benefit**: Can extend pavement life by 3 to 5 years
- **Customer Value Proposition**: "Your pavement is starting to show wear on the surface. Seal coating now will protect it from further deterioration and save you from more expensive repairs later."

**Moderate Stage** (Noticeable aggregate loss, rough surface, widespread):
- **Repair**: Thin overlay (1 to 1.5 inches)
  - Mill if existing surface is very rough (usually not necessary)
  - Tack coat the existing surface
  - Place thin overlay of new asphalt
  - Compact properly
- **Why**: At this stage, seal coating won't provide adequate protection. The surface is too deteriorated. An overlay provides a new wearing surface.
- **Cost**: $2 to $3 per square foot

**Severe Stage** (Deep raveling, significant aggregate loss, binder deterioration):
- **Repair**: Mill and resurface or full-depth patch
  - Mill out the raveled layer (typically 1.5 to 2 inches minimum, or deeper if raveling goes deep)
  - Verify underlying pavement is sound
  - Place new surface course
- **Why**: Overlaying severely raveled pavement without milling creates a rough, weak surface. The raveled material won't provide adequate support.
- **Cost**: $3 to $5+ per square foot depending on depth

**Special Case—Localized Raveling** (around utility covers, handwork areas, edges):
- This is often due to poor compaction or cold mix in those areas
- **Repair**: Remove the affected area (saw cut cleanly), replace with hot mix, compact properly
- **Prevention**: Pay special attention to compaction around structures and edges during construction

**Prevention**:

The best way to prevent raveling is regular maintenance:

1. **Seal Coating**: Every 3 to 5 years, depending on traffic and climate. This protects the binder from oxidation and extends life significantly.

2. **Proper Compaction**: During construction, ensure proper density. Use proper rolling techniques, check density with gauges or cores.

3. **Adequate Binder Content**: Use mixes with sufficient binder for the application. Don't cut corners with "lean" mixes in wearing courses.

4. **Timely Repairs**: Repair cracks promptly to prevent water infiltration, which accelerates raveling.

**Customer Communication**:
"This surface wear is caused by aging of the asphalt binder. It's been protecting your pavement for [X] years, and now it's time for maintenance. Seal coating will protect the surface and prevent further deterioration. If we wait too long, we'll have to do a more expensive mill and overlay."

Early intervention saves customers money. Position yourself as a partner in pavement management, not just a contractor who shows up when things fail.

### 7.3.2 Potholes

**What It Looks Like**

Potholes are bowl-shaped holes in the pavement surface, ranging from a few inches to several feet in diameter. They represent complete disintegration of the pavement in a localized area. Potholes often have rough, broken edges and may expose the base layer below. In wet conditions, potholes often pump water or mud when vehicles drive through them.

**Why It Happens**

Potholes don't just appear overnight—they're the end result of a process that often starts with minor distress:

**Water Infiltration Through Cracks**:
- Water enters the pavement structure through cracks (any type of crack—alligator, block, longitudinal, transverse)
- Water sitting under the pavement weakens the pavement structure and saturates the base

**Freeze-Thaw Cycles**:
- In cold climates, water in cracks and beneath pavement freezes
- When water freezes, it expands (about 9% volume increase)
- This expansion creates pressure that pushes the pavement up slightly and opens cracks wider
- When the ice melts, the water drains out, leaving a void
- The pavement settles into the void, creating a weakness

**Traffic Weakening of Compromised Area**:
- Once a crack or weak area exists, traffic repeatedly flexes that spot
- Each vehicle loosens more material
- Eventually, chunks of pavement break away, creating a pothole
- More traffic accelerates the process

**Poor Drainage**:
- Areas where water ponds or where drainage is inadequate are prime pothole candidates
- Standing water saturates the pavement structure
- Traffic on saturated pavement is especially destructive

**Sequence**: Crack → Water infiltration → Freeze-thaw or saturation → Weakening → Traffic → Pothole

**Why This Matters for Your Business**

Potholes are urgent issues. They damage vehicles, create liability for property owners, and are highly visible. When a customer calls about potholes, they want them fixed now.

1. **Emergency Repair Opportunity**: Many contractors offer pothole patching services with quick response time. This is a good revenue stream and builds customer relationships.

2. **Liability**: If potholes exist on property you maintain, you may be expected to repair them promptly under your maintenance contract. Make sure your contract language covers pothole repair scope and response time.

3. **Diagnostic Opportunity**: Potholes don't happen in isolation. If there are potholes, there are likely other pavement distresses nearby. Walk the site and look for the underlying cause: poor drainage, extensive cracking, aging pavement. Offer a comprehensive solution, not just pothole filling.

**How to Fix It**

Pothole repair quality varies widely. Done right, a pothole patch can last years. Done wrong, it fails in weeks.

**Proper Pothole Patching Procedure**:

1. **Remove Loose Material and Debris**:
   - Clean out the pothole completely
   - Remove any loose asphalt chunks, dirt, vegetation, or water
   - The edges should be sound—if the edge is weak or crumbling, square out the pothole further until you reach sound pavement
   - Use a broom, shovel, or compressed air

2. **Tack Coat Application**:
   - Apply asphalt emulsion tack coat to the sides and bottom of the pothole
   - This ensures bonding between the patch and the existing pavement
   - Don't skip this step—it's the difference between a patch that lasts and one that fails
   - Can use liquid tack coat or spray tack from a hand-held sprayer

3. **Hot Mix Asphalt Patching**:
   - Use hot mix asphalt (HMA) whenever possible—it's far superior to cold patch
   - Fill the pothole in lifts if it's deep (no more than 4 inches per lift)
   - Overfill slightly to account for compaction (typically 1/4 to 1/2 inch above surrounding pavement)
   - "Throw and go" doesn't work—you need proper material, placement, and compaction

4. **Proper Compaction**:
   - Compact each lift with a vibratory plate compactor, roller, or (for small potholes) hand tamper
   - For larger potholes, use a roller if available
   - Compact from the edges toward the center to confine the material
   - Check for density—the patch should be firm and should not compress further under traffic
   - After compaction, the patch should be level with or slightly above the surrounding pavement (to account for further compaction under traffic)

5. **Full-Depth Repair Preferred**:
   - If the base is visible at the bottom of the pothole, or if the base is weak, repair the base first
   - Remove unsuitable base material
   - Recompact or add new base material
   - Then place asphalt in lifts
   - Full-depth repairs last much longer than surface-only patches

**Why Proper Procedure Matters**:
- Potholes that are just "filled" without cleaning, tack coat, and compaction fail quickly—often within weeks
- Properly repaired potholes can last years
- Repeated pothole failures damage customer relationships and cost you profit margin on rework

**Cold Patch (Emergency Temporary Repair)**:
- Cold patch (asphalt in bags) can be used for winter or temporary emergency repairs
- **Limitations**: 
  - Cold patch doesn't compact as well as HMA
  - Cold patch doesn't bond as well
  - Cold patch is much more expensive per ton than HMA
- **When to Use**: Emergency repairs in winter, remote locations, or when hot mix isn't available
- **Follow-Up**: Permanent hot mix repair should replace cold patch when conditions allow

**Prevention**:

Potholes are preventable:

1. **Crack Sealing**: Seal cracks promptly to prevent water infiltration. This is the number one prevention measure.

2. **Drainage Maintenance**: Keep drainage systems clear and functioning. Eliminate standing water.

3. **Timely Repairs**: Address other distresses (alligator cracking, raveling) before they progress to potholes.

4. **Seal Coating**: Protects pavement from water infiltration and oxidation.

**Customer Communication**:
"Potholes form when water gets into the pavement through cracks, weakens the structure, and traffic breaks out chunks. We'll clean out the pothole, apply tack coat for bonding, fill it with hot mix, and compact it properly. This is a permanent repair. To prevent future potholes, we should also seal the other cracks on your property and address the drainage issue in the southwest corner."

This positions you as a problem-solver, not just a pothole filler.

## 7.4 Drainage-Related Issues

Poor drainage is the silent killer of asphalt pavements. It's not a distress itself, but it's the underlying cause of many distresses: alligator cracking, edge cracking, potholes, base failures, raveling, and more. Understanding drainage problems and how to fix them is critical to delivering pavements that last.

### Poor Drainage Problems

**Standing Water / Ponding**:
- Water that doesn't drain off the pavement surface or through the pavement structure
- Visible after rain—puddles that remain for hours or days
- **Causes**:
  - Inadequate slope (less than 1% to 2%)
  - Depressions or birdbaths in pavement
  - Blocked or inadequate drainage inlets
  - Poor grading around pavement edges

**Accelerated Pavement Deterioration**:
- Water sitting on pavement accelerates oxidation and aging of the binder
- Freeze-thaw cycles in standing water cause more damage than cycles in well-drained areas
- Standing water seeps into cracks and accelerates their deterioration

**Base Saturation and Weakening**:
- Water infiltrating through cracks, joints, or edges saturates the base and subgrade
- Saturated granular base materials lose much of their load-bearing capacity (can drop 50% or more)
- Repeated traffic on saturated base causes pumping—fine particles and water are ejected through cracks under wheel loads
- Pumping creates voids under the pavement, leading to settlement and structural failure

**Safety Hazards**:
- Standing water creates hydroplaning hazards
- Ice accumulation in winter
- Mosquito breeding in warm climates

**Why This Matters for Your Business**

When you're evaluating a pavement for repair or proposing maintenance, always assess drainage:

1. **Root Cause Analysis**: If you're seeing extensive cracking, potholes, or structural failures, investigate drainage. You may be treating symptoms while ignoring the cause. If drainage isn't corrected, your repairs will fail prematurely.

2. **Scope Expansion**: Drainage corrections can significantly increase project scope and cost. Identify drainage issues during your site walk and include them in your proposal. Don't discover drainage problems halfway through construction.

3. **Warranty Protection**: If drainage issues exist and the customer declines to address them, document it. Your warranty shouldn't cover failures caused by drainage problems you identified but the owner chose not to fix.

### Solutions

**Improve Drainage Systems**:

**Surface Drainage**:
- Ensure proper slope: minimum 2% (1/4 inch per foot) for most applications
  - In areas where 2% can't be achieved (ADA requirements, site constraints), 1% is absolute minimum
- Eliminate depressions and birdbaths by patching or mill-and-overlay
- Crown roadways and parking lots to promote drainage to edges
- Create swales or ditches to carry water away from pavement edges

**Subsurface Drainage**:
- **Install or Repair Inlets and Pipes**:
  - Catch basins in low points to collect surface water
  - Trench drains in areas where surface drainage is difficult
  - Storm sewer pipes to convey water away
  - Clean out clogged inlets and pipes
  - Repair or replace broken pipes

**Underdrains for Subsurface Water**:
- For pavements with base saturation problems, subsurface drainage is essential
- Install edge drains (perforated pipe in gravel-filled trenches along pavement edges) to lower the water table under the pavement
- Typical edge drain: 4-inch perforated pipe in 6-inch wide trench, backfilled with drainage aggregate, wrapped in geotextile fabric
- Underdrains discharge to daylight or storm sewer
- **Why This Works**: Keeps base and subgrade dry, maintaining load-bearing capacity

**Reshape Surface for Positive Drainage**:
- In rehabilitation projects, adjust grades to eliminate ponding
- May require additional asphalt in low areas, or milling high areas
- Consider full mill-and-overlay to re-establish proper drainage slope
- On large projects, may need surveying to design proper drainage grades

**Base and Subgrade Improvements**:
- Open-graded base materials drain better than dense-graded base
- In areas with high water tables or poor subgrade, consider stabilizing the subgrade or using geotextiles to separate subgrade from base
- Ensure base extends beyond pavement edge to prevent water infiltration at the edge

**Preventive Measures in New Construction**:

Designing proper drainage from the start prevents problems:

1. **Grade Design**: Design minimum 2% slope from day one. Don't accept site designs with flat areas or reverse slopes.

2. **Drainage Structures**: Include adequate inlets and piping in the design. Space inlets appropriately (typically 300 to 600 feet apart on roadways, closer in parking lots).

3. **Base Material Selection**: Use free-draining base materials where appropriate.

4. **Edge Details**: Extend base beyond pavement edge; provide edge drains in areas with poor subsurface drainage.

**Customer Communication**:

When you identify drainage problems, explain them clearly:

"Water is the enemy of asphalt pavement. These low areas where water stands are accelerating pavement deterioration. We can fix the surface distresses, but if we don't correct the drainage, the problems will come back. I recommend we [specific drainage solution]. This adds [$X] to the project but ensures the repairs will last."

Or, if the customer declines:

"I understand budget is a concern. We can repair the surface distresses for the amount quoted, but I need to note that the drainage issues in [location] may cause premature failure. If you'd like to address drainage later, we can do that as a separate project."

Documentation protects you and helps customers make informed decisions.

## 7.5 Pavement Evaluation and Assessment

Before you can fix a pavement problem, you need to properly diagnose it. Pavement evaluation is the process of systematically assessing pavement condition to identify distresses, determine their causes and severity, and develop appropriate repair strategies. This is a skill that separates professional contractors from "pavers with a truck."

### Visual Inspection Methods

The most basic—and often most valuable—evaluation tool is a thorough visual inspection. You don't need expensive equipment to identify most pavement distresses.

**Distress Identification and Mapping**:

Walk the entire pavement area systematically. For parking lots, walk each row. For roadways, walk the full length in both directions. Look for:
- **Cracking**: What type? Where? How extensive?
- **Deformation**: Rutting, shoving, depressions?
- **Disintegration**: Raveling, potholes?
- **Edge Condition**: Edge cracking, edge drop-off, vegetation encroachment?
- **Drainage**: Standing water? Visible drainage problems?
- **Ride Quality**: Rough areas, bumps, unevenness?

**Document Your Findings**:
- Take photos (date-stamped if possible)
- Sketch a site plan and mark distress locations
- Use a measuring wheel or tape to quantify extent
- Note any patterns (all wheel paths, all north-facing areas, near drainage inlets, etc.)

**Severity Rating Systems**:

Rate each distress by severity. A simple three-level system works well:

**Low Severity**:
- Cracks are tight (less than 1/4 inch wide)
- No spalling at crack edges
- Minimal surface loss
- Does not affect ride quality significantly
- Example: Early-stage block cracking, tight longitudinal cracks

**Moderate Severity**:
- Cracks are wider (1/4 to 1/2 inch) with some spalling
- Some loss of material
- Noticeable but not severe impact on ride quality
- Example: Moderate raveling, interconnected cracking with some breakup, shallow rutting

**High Severity**:
- Cracks are wide (over 1/2 inch) with significant spalling and breakup
- Extensive material loss
- Significant impact on ride quality or safety
- Example: Potholes, severe alligator cracking with loose pieces, deep rutting

**Extent Quantification**:

Estimate the extent of each distress:
- For cracking: linear feet or percentage of area affected
- For rutting: affected lane length
- For raveling: square feet or percentage of area
- For potholes: count and total area

This quantification is essential for accurate estimating.

**Why This Matters for Your Business**:

A thorough visual inspection demonstrates professionalism:
- Customers appreciate contractors who take time to assess the problem rather than just quoting a standard $/sq ft price
- Accurate severity and extent data leads to accurate estimates
- Documentation protects you if scope expands or if disputes arise
- A comprehensive inspection often reveals problems the customer didn't know about, creating opportunities for additional work

**Practical Tip**: Walk the site with the customer when possible. Point out problems as you see them. Explain what you're seeing and why it matters. This educates the customer and builds trust.

### Pavement Condition Index (PCI)

PCI is a standardized numerical rating system for pavement condition. It provides a single number (0 to 100) that represents overall pavement condition, based on the type, severity, and extent of distresses observed.

**PCI Scale**:
- **85-100**: Good condition—preventive maintenance only
- **70-85**: Satisfactory—minor rehabilitation or continued maintenance
- **55-70**: Fair—more significant rehabilitation needed
- **40-55**: Poor—major rehabilitation required
- **25-40**: Very Poor—reconstruction may be more cost-effective than rehabilitation
- **0-25**: Failed—reconstruction necessary

**How PCI is Determined**:

PCI follows a standardized procedure (ASTM D6433):
1. Divide pavement into sample units (typically 1,000 to 10,000 sq ft sections)
2. Inspect sample units and record distress types, severities, and quantities
3. Calculate deduct values for each distress based on type, severity, and extent
4. Sum deduct values and convert to PCI using standard curves
5. Average across all sample units to get overall PCI

**Why Use PCI**:
- **Objective**: Less subjective than "good/fair/poor" ratings
- **Standardized**: Allows comparison across projects and over time
- **Communication Tool**: A single number is easy for customers to understand
- **Maintenance Prioritization**: Helps determine which pavements need attention first

**Practical Application**:

Full PCI evaluations require training and are time-consuming. For smaller projects, a simplified approach works:
- Walk the site and identify major distresses
- Rate severity (low/moderate/high)
- Estimate extent
- Use your experience to assign an approximate PCI range

For larger projects, consider partnering with a pavement engineering firm that can perform formal PCI evaluations.

**Why This Matters for Your Business**:
- For municipal or institutional customers managing multiple facilities, speaking the language of PCI makes you more credible
- PCI data helps customers justify maintenance budgets and plan multi-year programs
- Tracking PCI over time demonstrates the value of maintenance programs you provide

### Structural Evaluation

Visual inspection tells you what distresses exist on the surface. Structural evaluation tells you what's happening underneath—the strength and remaining life of the pavement structure.

**When is Structural Evaluation Needed?**:
- When deciding between overlay and reconstruction
- When pavement shows signs of structural failure (alligator cracking, pumping)
- For major rehabilitation projects
- For developing long-term pavement management programs
- For forensic investigations (why did this pavement fail?)

**Structural Evaluation Methods**:

**Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD) Testing**:
- **What It Is**: A device that drops a weight onto the pavement and measures how much the pavement deflects (bends)
- **Why It's Useful**: 
  - Deflection indicates structural capacity—weak pavements deflect more
  - Pattern of deflection at multiple sensors identifies which layer is weak (surface, base, subgrade)
  - Can estimate remaining pavement life
  - Helps determine if overlay is feasible or if reconstruction is necessary
- **Practical Note**: FWD testing requires specialized equipment and expertise. Most contractors don't own FWD equipment but can hire consultants for testing.

**Ground Penetrating Radar (GPR)**:
- **What It Is**: Radar waves sent through pavement to detect layer thicknesses and subsurface features
- **Why It's Useful**:
  - Non-destructive—doesn't damage pavement
  - Can scan large areas quickly
  - Identifies layer thicknesses, voids, moisture, and deterioration
  - Helps locate utilities before cutting
- **Practical Note**: Also requires specialized equipment and interpretation expertise

**Coring and Layer Evaluation**:
- **What It Is**: Drilling cylindrical cores through the pavement to examine layers
- **Why It's Useful**:
  - Directly measures asphalt thickness
  - Shows layer condition, bonding between layers, mix quality
  - Can test core samples in lab for density, asphalt content, aggregate gradation
  - Identifies base and subgrade materials
- **Practical Note**: This is the most accessible structural evaluation method—many contractors have coring equipment or can rent it
- **Procedure**:
  1. Core at representative locations (distressed areas, good areas, edges)
  2. Measure layer thicknesses
  3. Visually evaluate condition of each layer
  4. Check for stripping, raveling, poor bonding, base contamination
  5. Lab testing if needed

**Remaining Life Estimation**:

Based on distress severity, structural capacity, and traffic loading, you can estimate how many years of service the pavement has left:
- Pavements with low PCI and high deflections may have only 1 to 3 years remaining
- Pavements with moderate distress and good structural capacity may have 5 to 10 years remaining
- Good condition pavements with high structural capacity can last 15+ years with proper maintenance

This helps customers decide: "Should I spend $X on rehabilitation now to get 15 more years, or do a cheaper maintenance fix to get 3 more years and then reconstruct?"

**Why This Matters for Your Business**:

Structural evaluation capabilities—even basic coring—set you apart:
- You can provide data-driven recommendations rather than just opinions
- Customers trust contractors who take time to investigate, not just quote
- Structural evaluation data protects you—if you core and find the pavement is only 2 inches thick instead of the 4 inches the customer thought, you can adjust your scope and price before committing
- For large projects, recommending professional structural evaluation shows you're serious about quality

**Practical Approach for Small Contractors**:

You don't need to own an FWD or GPR, but you should:
1. **Learn to use a core drill** or partner with a testing lab
2. **Document visual distresses** systematically
3. **Take lots of photos** (before, during, after)
4. **Keep records** of your projects—what you did, what the distresses were, what the repair cost, how long it lasted

Over time, you build experience that lets you diagnose problems quickly and accurately.

## Closing Thoughts on Chapter 7

If you've made it through this chapter, you now have a systematic framework for diagnosing, understanding, and repairing asphalt pavement distresses. This is not academic knowledge—this is the core competency that lets you deliver value, protect your warranty exposure, and build a reputation as a contractor who knows what he's doing.

**Key Takeaways**:

1. **Accurate Diagnosis is Essential**: Different distresses have different causes and require different repairs. Misdiagnose the problem, and you'll waste time and money on repairs that don't work. Take time to understand what you're seeing.

2. **Address Root Causes, Not Just Symptoms**: Crack sealing alligator cracks without fixing the structural problem underneath is throwing money away. Patching potholes without addressing drainage is setting yourself up for more potholes. Always ask "Why did this happen?" and fix that.

3. **Communicate Clearly with Customers**: Explain what you're seeing, why it happened, what needs to be done, and what it will cost. Give customers options when appropriate. Honest, clear communication builds trust and long-term relationships.

4. **Documentation Protects You**: Take photos, keep records, write clear proposals that define scope. If drainage problems exist and the customer declines to fix them, document it. If you discover conditions are worse than expected, document it before proceeding.

5. **Preventive Maintenance is Always Cheaper**: Help your customers understand that regular maintenance—crack sealing, seal coating, drainage maintenance—costs a fraction of rehabilitation or reconstruction. Position yourself as a partner in pavement management, not just a contractor who shows up when things fail.

6. **Quality Construction Prevents Problems**: Proper compaction, adequate thickness, appropriate mix design, good drainage, and attention to detail during construction prevent most of the problems covered in this chapter. Every shortcut you take during construction creates a future repair opportunity—but also a future warranty claim and an unhappy customer.

The contractors who excel in this industry are the ones who can walk a property, quickly identify and diagnose distresses, explain them clearly to the customer, and propose solutions that address root causes. You're now equipped to do that.

In the next chapters, we'll move into specialized considerations—climate adaptations, sustainability, safety, and more. But troubleshooting and repair work will always be a core part of your business. Master these skills, and you'll build a reputation that brings customers back and generates referrals.

Stay sharp, keep learning, and let's keep moving forward.
