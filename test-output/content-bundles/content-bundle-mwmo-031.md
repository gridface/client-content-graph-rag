Understanding these demands helps property owners specify, construct, and maintain pavement that supports rather than constrains their operations.

## E-Commerce Operations: Different Demands, Different Design

Traditional warehousing and modern e-commerce fulfillment place fundamentally different demands on facility pavement. Understanding these differences drives appropriate specification.

Trailer volume and turnover have increased dramatically with e-commerce growth. Traditional warehouses might see a dozen trailer movements daily. Modern fulfillment centers processing e-commerce orders may see hundreds of trailer arrivals and departures across multiple shifts. This dramatically higher traffic volume accelerates pavement wear and demands more robust structural sections.

Delivery truck diversity has expanded beyond traditional tractor-trailers. E-commerce operations receive and ship via everything from full-size eighteen-wheelers to box trucks, sprinter vans, and even passenger vehicles for last-mile delivery services. This mix of vehicle types creates varied loading patterns that pavement must accommodate. Design must address both the heaviest vehicles (full tractor-trailers) and the frequent lighter traffic that cumulatively adds loading.

Operating hours extend toward continuous operations at many e-commerce facilities. Twenty-four-hour operations mean pavement never gets extended rest periods. Nighttime operations often coincide with cooler temperatures that affect asphalt workability during maintenance. Summer operations during peak heat expose pavement to maximum thermal stress during highest traffic periods.

Speed and efficiency pressures translate to faster vehicle movements through truck courts and loading areas. Drivers maneuvering under time pressure accelerate and brake more aggressively than traditional warehouse operations. These dynamic loads create stress patterns that purely static loading analysis would underestimate.

## Truck Court and Loading Dock Design

The truck court—the maneuvering area between the warehouse face and the property perimeter—represents the highest-stress zone in warehouse pavement design. Proper design ensures efficient operations while delivering decades of service.

Heavy loading concentrations occur where trailers back into dock positions. As tractors disconnect and trailers drop onto landing gear, the entire trailer weight concentrates on relatively small contact patches. This standing load remains in place for hours or days while trailers are loaded or unloaded. Design must address these concentrated static loads through adequate pavement thickness and rut-resistant materials.

Maneuvering zone traffic patterns create specific stress distributions. Trucks entering the truck court follow relatively consistent paths toward their assigned doors. These approach lanes see concentrated wheel path loading. Backing maneuvers spread loading more broadly but create high horizontal shear stresses as tires pivot during tight turns. Departure routes concentrate loading in different wheel paths than approaches.

Recommended structural sections for truck courts typically include six to eight inches of asphalt over eight to twelve inches of aggregate base. The asphalt should be placed in multiple lifts (commonly three-inch or four-inch lifts) to achieve proper compaction in each layer. PG 70-22 binder provides enhanced rutting resistance compared to standard grades—essential for the standing loads and slow-moving traffic typical of truck courts.

Dock approach aprons warrant enhanced design beyond even truck court standards. The immediate area in front of dock doors sees the highest loading concentration, including trailer landing gear loads, dock leveler impacts, and concentrated wheel loads from delivery equipment. Some specifications call for concrete aprons in these highest-stress zones, with asphalt transitions to the broader truck court area.

## Employee and Visitor Parking

Warehouse and distribution facilities typically require substantial employee parking to support operations across multiple shifts. While lighter-duty than truck areas, employee parking still warrants proper design attention.

Traffic volume calculations should account for shift-change patterns. Facilities running multiple shifts may see their entire parking area cycle twice or three times daily—far more vehicle movements than the total parking count might suggest. Peak shift changes concentrate traffic that creates specific loading patterns in circulation aisles.

Standard commercial parking specifications apply to most employee areas. Four inches of asphalt over six inches of aggregate base handles passenger vehicle traffic adequately. Drive aisles serving parking areas may warrant slightly enhanced sections (five inches over six to eight inches base) to handle the concentrated circulation traffic.

Visitor and administrative parking near building entrances often benefits from enhanced aesthetic considerations. Fresh sealcoating, crisp striping, and well-maintained surfaces create positive first impressions for customers, vendors, and prospective employees. These areas may warrant more frequent maintenance cycles than general employee parking.

Delivery vehicle parking zones—areas where box trucks, vans, or company vehicles stage—need heavier design than passenger vehicle areas. These zones often fall between employee parking and truck court specifications. Five to six inches of asphalt over six to eight inches of base typically provides adequate capacity.

## Climate Considerations for Northwest Arkansas

The region's climate creates specific challenges for warehouse pavement that design and construction must address.

Temperature ranges in Northwest Arkansas span from summer highs exceeding ninety-five degrees to winter lows below zero. This temperature swing creates thermal stress cycles that all pavement must accommodate. Proper binder grade selection—PG 64-22 or PG 70-22 depending on loading—ensures adequate performance across this temperature range.

Freeze-thaw cycling, while less severe than northern states, still affects the region. Water infiltration through cracks or joints freezes and expands during cold periods, then melts and leaves voids. The cumulative effect of repeated freeze-thaw cycles accelerates pavement deterioration. Preventing water infiltration through proper drainage and timely crack sealing minimizes this damage.

Seasonal moisture patterns affect both construction timing and long-term performance. Spring often brings extended wet periods that complicate construction scheduling and can saturate subgrade materials. Summer thunderstorms can interrupt paving operations and deposit standing water that must drain before traffic can resume. Fall typically offers the most favorable paving weather in the region.

Ice storms, while infrequent, create specific challenges for warehouse operations. Facilities must balance pavement protection against operational necessity during ice events. De-icing chemicals can accelerate certain pavement distresses—understanding these tradeoffs helps facility managers make informed decisions during winter weather.

## Construction Quality for Warehouse Longevity

Warehouse pavement represents significant capital investment—often hundreds of thousands of dollars for major distribution facilities. Construction quality determines whether that investment delivers its expected service life.

Subgrade evaluation and preparation form the foundation for all subsequent construction. Northwest Arkansas soils vary considerably across the region, with some areas featuring stable granular materials and others presenting plastic clays that are moisture-sensitive. Geotechnical evaluation identifies subgrade characteristics and guides appropriate preparation. Soft spots, organic material, or high-plasticity soils must be addressed before base placement—problems in the subgrade will eventually manifest as pavement failures regardless of surface construction quality.

Base construction requires appropriate material quality and compaction. Crushed aggregate base should meet specified gradation requirements with adequate fines for compaction but not excessive fines that become unstable when wet. Compaction to ninety-five percent of maximum dry density should be verified through testing. Proof rolling—running a loaded truck across the completed base—identifies soft areas that might escape density testing.

Asphalt placement temperature and timing affect achieved density. Material should arrive at temperatures between 275°F and 325°F for conventional hot mix and remain above 175°F through final compaction. Delays that allow material to cool below compaction temperature result in density deficiencies that manifest as premature wear and raveling.

Joint construction quality particularly affects warehouse pavement longevity. Longitudinal joints between adjacent paving passes will be visible throughout the pavement's life and represent structural weak points. Proper joint construction—including correct notching, adequate tack coat application, tight matching of adjacent lanes, and thorough compaction—minimizes joint-related failures.

Surface smoothness matters for warehouse operations beyond aesthetics. Rough surfaces slow forklift operations, damage goods in transit, and create safety concerns. Proper paver operation, roller patterns, and attention to finish grade produce surfaces that support efficient operations.

## Maintenance Planning for Distribution Facilities

Proactive maintenance extends pavement life and prevents minor issues from becoming operational disruptions. For warehouse facilities running continuous operations, planned maintenance beats emergency repairs.

Crack sealing should begin when cracks appear—typically within two to four years for thermal cracking in truck court areas. Hot-pour rubberized sealant prevents water infiltration that accelerates base deterioration. Annual crack sealing cycles keep maintenance manageable and prevent the water damage that leads to potholes and structural failures.

Pothole repair demands prompt attention in warehouse operations. A pothole in a truck court creates immediate safety and operational concerns. Proper pothole repair—not temporary fill but full-depth patching with hot mix asphalt—restores structural integrity. Emergency repair capability should be identified before incidents occur.

Periodic assessment identifies developing problems before they impact operations. Quarterly inspections of truck courts, dock aprons, and high-traffic circulation routes catch distress early. Documentation through photos and notes supports maintenance planning and contractor accountability.

Pavement management planning takes assessment data and projects future repair needs. Understanding when major rehabilitation or reconstruction will be needed allows budgeting and scheduling that minimizes operational impact. Facilities that plan for pavement lifecycle costs make better investment decisions than those that react to failures.
