# CHAPTER 3: PAVEMENT DESIGN AND ENGINEERING

In Chapters 1 and 2, we built your foundation—understanding what asphalt is made of, how different mixes work, and when to use each type. Now we're going to talk about pavement design: how thick does the pavement need to be, what goes underneath it, and how do we engineer a pavement structure that will carry the expected loads for the intended service life?

This is where contractors often get nervous. Pavement design sounds like engineering school—lots of formulas, technical terms, and calculations. But here's the reality: you don't need to be able to design a pavement from scratch (that's what engineers are for), but you absolutely need to understand the principles behind pavement design. Why? Because this knowledge helps you:

- **Verify that designs are reasonable** and catch obvious problems before they become expensive failures
- **Explain to customers** why you're recommending specific thicknesses and not just guessing
- **Adjust bids intelligently** when soil conditions differ from plans
- **Troubleshoot problems** when pavements don't perform as expected
- **Have credible conversations** with engineers, inspectors, and customers

When you can walk a property owner through the design logic—"Based on your soil conditions, traffic volume, and expected service life, we need X inches of asphalt over Y inches of base"—you're demonstrating expertise that separates you from competitors who just say "We'll put down 3 inches of asphalt and it'll be fine."

Let's demystify pavement design and make it a tool that helps you win better jobs and deliver better value.

## 3.1 Structural Design Fundamentals

Pavement design is fundamentally about one thing: distributing loads from the surface down to the subgrade soil in a way that prevents failure. Think of it like this: when a heavy truck drives over pavement, all that weight is concentrated on a few square inches of tire contact. The pavement's job is to spread that load out over a larger area so the soil underneath can support it without deforming or failing.

### Design Philosophy

#### Traffic Load Distribution

Here's the basic concept: loads at the surface need to be spread out before they reach the subgrade.

**The Load Spreading Principle**:
- A truck tire might exert 100 psi on the pavement surface
- The asphalt layers spread this load laterally as it goes deeper
- By the time the load reaches the top of the base, it might be 50 psi over a larger area
- By the time it reaches the subgrade, it might be 10-15 psi over an even larger area
- As long as the stress on the subgrade is below the soil's bearing capacity, the pavement won't fail

**Why Thickness Matters**: 
- Thicker pavement = more load spreading = less stress on subgrade
- Weak subgrade soils need thicker pavements to adequately spread loads
- Strong subgrade soils can support pavements with less thickness

**The Layered System Concept**: We use multiple layers because:
- Different materials have different strengths and costs
- Asphalt (strongest, most expensive) goes on top where loads are highest
- Aggregate base (strong, moderate cost) goes in the middle
- Subbase (moderate strength, lower cost) may be used to improve weak soils
- Subgrade (existing soil, no cost but often weak) is at the bottom

This layered approach lets us optimize cost and performance. You don't need 12 inches of asphalt to carry heavy loads—4 inches of asphalt over 8 inches of quality base can do the same job at lower cost.

**Business Application**: When explaining pavement design to customers, this load distribution concept helps them understand why "thicker is better" isn't always the answer. Sometimes it's more cost-effective to improve the base or stabilize the subgrade than to just add more asphalt.

#### Environmental Considerations

Pavement doesn't just carry loads—it also has to survive the environment.

**Temperature Extremes**:
- **Heat**: Causes asphalt to soften, making it susceptible to rutting
- **Cold**: Causes asphalt to stiffen and potentially crack under stress
- **Daily Cycles**: Repeated heating and cooling causes expansion/contraction stresses
- **Seasonal Cycles**: Freeze-thaw cycles can be particularly destructive

**Moisture Effects**:
- Water weakens subgrade and base materials
- Freezing water expands, creating frost heave
- Wet materials lose strength and are more easily deformed
- Water infiltration accelerates pavement aging and damage

**Design Responses to Environment**:
- Binder selection matched to climate (PG system we discussed in Chapter 1)
- Drainage design to remove water quickly
- Thicker pavements in freeze-thaw areas
- Base stabilization in wet climates
- Consideration of seasonal load restrictions during spring thaw

**Regional Differences**: A pavement design that works perfectly in Arizona might fail in Alaska. Temperature ranges, freeze-thaw cycles, moisture conditions—all affect how we design pavements.

**Why This Matters for Your Business**: Understanding environmental factors helps you:
- Recognize when a "cookie-cutter" design won't work for local conditions
- Recommend appropriate modifications based on site-specific conditions
- Avoid warranties on pavements designed for the wrong climate
- Explain to customers why proper drainage and binder selection are worth the investment

#### Performance Period (Typically 20 Years)

Pavements are designed for a specific service life—the number of years the pavement is expected to perform adequately before major rehabilitation is needed.

**Common Performance Periods**:
- **Residential driveways**: 10-15 years
- **Commercial parking lots**: 15-20 years
- **Municipal streets**: 20-30 years
- **Major highways**: 20-40 years
- **Airport pavements**: 20-40 years

**What "Performance" Means**: It doesn't mean the pavement will be perfect for 20 years and then suddenly fail. It means:
- Minimal cracking for most of the design life
- No rutting beyond acceptable limits
- Acceptable ride quality
- Manageable maintenance costs
- At the end of design life, rehabilitation (like overlay) becomes necessary

**Design Life vs. Service Life**:
- **Design Life**: What the pavement was engineered for
- **Actual Service Life**: How long it actually lasts (affected by maintenance, actual traffic, construction quality)

With proper maintenance (crack sealing, seal coating, timely repairs), a 20-year design life can often extend to 25-30 years of actual service.

**The Economics of Design Life**: Here's where this matters for your business:

A customer might say: "Why should I pay for a 20-year design when I'm only going to own this property for 10 years?"

Your answer: "Three reasons. First, a properly designed pavement will need minimal maintenance during those 10 years, saving you money. Second, when you sell, a well-maintained pavement adds value—buyers will pay more for a property that doesn't need immediate paving work. Third, underdesigning now means you'll likely need expensive repairs in 5-6 years instead of simple maintenance, which could happen on your watch."

**Practical Note**: Most specifications and codes require specific design periods. For commercial work, 20 years is standard. For residential driveways, 10-15 years is common. Don't promise longer design life than what you're building for—set realistic expectations.

### Design Methods

There are several approaches to pavement design, each with different levels of complexity and accuracy.

#### Empirical Methods

Empirical methods are based on observed performance of existing pavements. They answer the question: "What thickness worked well on similar projects?"

**How They Work**:
- Based on decades of observed pavement performance
- Correlate design inputs (traffic, soil conditions) with thickness requirements
- Use charts, tables, or simple formulas
- Don't attempt to predict exactly how pavements will behave—just what has worked in the past

**Advantages**:
- Relatively simple to use
- Don't require sophisticated testing
- Proven track record—we know they work
- Good for routine projects

**Limitations**:
- Only applicable to conditions similar to those where data was collected
- Don't account for new materials or technologies
- Conservative (may overdesign to be safe)
- Can't predict specific types of distress

**Common Empirical Methods**:
- Asphalt Institute thickness design charts (MS-1 manual)
- State DOT standard sections
- Experience-based guidelines

**When Empirical Methods Are Appropriate**:
- Standard projects with typical conditions
- When historical performance data is available
- Budget or schedule doesn't allow for complex design
- Low-risk projects where overdesign isn't costly

**Business Application**: For most commercial parking lots, residential work, and routine municipal projects, empirical methods are perfectly adequate. You don't need sophisticated mechanistic-empirical design for a shopping center parking lot—the Asphalt Institute charts or a state DOT standard section will work fine and save design costs.

#### Mechanistic-Empirical Approaches

These methods combine mechanics (how materials respond to loads) with empirical data (observed performance).

**How They Work**:
- Calculate stresses and strains in pavement layers using engineering principles
- Predict how materials will respond to repeated loading
- Use empirical equations to relate stress/strain to pavement distress
- Can predict specific distress types (rutting, cracking, etc.)

**The MEPDG (Mechanistic-Empirical Pavement Design Guide)**: This is the state-of-the-art pavement design method, developed through a major research program in the 1990s-2000s.

**Advantages**:
- More accurate for unusual conditions
- Can predict specific performance issues
- Accounts for material properties, climate, traffic, and construction quality
- Can optimize designs to minimize cost while meeting performance targets

**Limitations**:
- Requires extensive material testing and characterization
- Needs specialized software (AASHTOWare Pavement ME Design)
- Requires engineering expertise to use properly
- More expensive and time-consuming than empirical methods

**When Mechanistic-Empirical Design Makes Sense**:
- Large, expensive projects where optimization saves significant money
- Unusual conditions (very heavy loads, challenging soils, harsh climate)
- New materials or innovative designs
- When precise performance prediction is important

**Your Role**: You won't be doing mechanistic-empirical design yourself—that's for engineers. But understanding that it exists helps you know when to recommend bringing in a professional engineer (complex projects) versus using standard empirical methods (routine work).

#### AASHTO Pavement Design Method

The American Association of State Highway and Transportation Officials (AASHTO) method is the most widely used empirical design approach in the United States.

**Origin**: Based on the AASHO Road Test conducted in the late 1950s—a massive experiment where trucks ran on test pavements until they failed. Researchers measured how different pavement thicknesses performed under known traffic loads.

**Key Concepts**:
- **Structural Number (SN)**: A measure of total pavement strength (we'll detail this in section 3.3)
- **ESAL**: Equivalent Single Axle Loads—a way to convert all different truck types into a common unit
- **Serviceability**: A measure of pavement ride quality and condition

**The Basic Process**:
1. Determine design traffic (in ESALs)
2. Characterize subgrade strength (CBR or resilient modulus)
3. Determine required Structural Number from design charts
4. Convert SN to layer thicknesses using layer coefficients

**Why AASHTO Design Is Important**:
- Most state DOT specifications are based on AASHTO
- If you're bidding municipal or state projects, you'll encounter AASHTO design
- It's the industry standard language for pavement design
- Understanding AASHTO helps you read and verify design plans

**Practical Note**: The full AASHTO design method is complex (there's a reason engineers spend semesters studying it). But understanding the basic concepts—Structural Number, ESALs, layer coefficients—helps you communicate with engineers and understand design plans.

#### Catalog Designs

Many agencies develop "catalog" or "standard" designs for typical applications.

**What They Are**:
- Pre-designed pavement sections for common situations
- Based on traffic levels, soil conditions, and local materials
- Approved for use without project-specific design

**Example Catalog Sections**:
- **Residential Street, Low Traffic**: 3" HMA over 6" aggregate base
- **Collector Street, Moderate Traffic**: 4" HMA over 8" aggregate base
- **Arterial Street, Heavy Traffic**: 5" HMA (2" surface + 3" base course) over 12" aggregate base
- **Commercial Parking Lot**: 4" HMA over 6" aggregate base

**Advantages**:
- Simple to specify and bid
- No design fees
- Proven performance in local conditions
- Fast plan review and approval
- Familiar to contractors and inspectors

**Limitations**:
- May not fit unusual conditions
- Can be overly conservative (expensive)
- Might not account for site-specific issues

**Business Application**: Catalog designs are your friend for routine work. If your local DOT or building department has standard sections for parking lots, driveways, or subdivision streets, use them. They're pre-approved, reviewers know them, and everybody understands what you're building. Save the custom engineering for projects where it's truly needed.

**When to Go Beyond Catalog Designs**:
- Soils significantly worse than typical (very soft clays, peat, organic soils)
- Traffic much heavier than typical
- Very large projects where optimization saves money
- Unusual constraints (limited thickness, poor drainage, etc.)

## 3.2 Pavement Structural Components

A pavement is a layered system, and each layer has a specific function. Understanding these layers helps you recognize problems, recommend solutions, and build pavements that perform.

Think of it like building a house: you need a strong foundation (subgrade), a level platform (base/subbase), and a finished surface (asphalt). Each component is critical. The most beautiful asphalt surface won't perform if it's sitting on weak subgrade or poorly compacted base.

### Subgrade

The subgrade is the in-place soil that everything else sits on. It's the foundation of your pavement system, and it's usually the weakest link.

#### Soil Classification Systems

Not all soils are created equal. Some soils are strong and stable; others are weak and problematic. We need a standard way to describe and classify soils so we know what we're dealing with.

**AASHTO Soil Classification System**: This is the most common system in highway work. It classifies soils into groups based on particle size and plasticity:

- **A-1, A-2**: Granular materials (gravels and sands)—good subgrade soils
- **A-3**: Fine sand—fair subgrade
- **A-4, A-5**: Silty soils—fair to poor subgrade
- **A-6, A-7**: Clayey soils—poor subgrade (A-7 is the worst)

Within each group, there are subgroups. For example:
- **A-1-a**: Stone fragments, gravel, and sand—excellent
- **A-2-6**: Gravelly or sandy clay—fair to good
- **A-7-6**: Clayey soil—poor (highly plastic clay)

**Unified Soil Classification System (USCS)**: This is another common system, often used in geotechnical reports:
- **GW, GP**: Well-graded or poorly-graded gravel—excellent
- **SW, SP**: Well-graded or poorly-graded sand—good to excellent
- **ML**: Silt—fair to poor
- **CL, CH**: Clay (low or high plasticity)—poor to very poor
- **OH**: Organic clay—very poor

**What You Need to Know**: When you see a soils report (geotechnical report), it will classify the subgrade soil. The classification tells you immediately whether you're dealing with good soil (A-1, GW) or problem soil (A-7, CH).

**Business Implication**: If plans show 4" of asphalt over 6" of base, but when you dig test pits you find A-7 clay instead of the A-3 sand shown on the plans, you've got a problem. That design won't work. You need to notify the owner and engineer immediately—don't proceed with the original design on bad soil. Document the conditions and get a design revision.

#### CBR Testing and Requirements

CBR (California Bearing Ratio) is the most common test for measuring subgrade strength.

**What Is CBR?**: It measures how much force is required to push a piston into soil compared to a standard crushed stone material. It's expressed as a percentage:
- **100% CBR**: As strong as the standard crushed stone
- **10% CBR**: 10% as strong as the standard
- **3% CBR**: Very weak soil (3% of standard strength)

**Typical CBR Values**:
- **80-100% CBR**: Excellent subgrade (well-graded gravel, crushed stone)
- **20-50% CBR**: Good subgrade (sand, sandy gravel)
- **10-20% CBR**: Fair subgrade (sandy silt, silty sand)
- **5-10% CBR**: Poor subgrade (silt, clay)
- **< 5% CBR**: Very poor subgrade (soft clay, organic soil, peat)

**How CBR Affects Design**: Weaker subgrade (lower CBR) requires thicker pavement to adequately distribute loads. Here's a rough guide:
- **CBR > 20%**: Standard pavement thickness
- **CBR 10-20%**: May need increased thickness or base stabilization
- **CBR 5-10%**: Definitely need increased thickness or subgrade treatment
- **CBR < 5%**: Serious subgrade problems—excavation, stabilization, or geotextiles needed

**Testing CBR**: 
- Lab testing: Soil samples are compacted and tested in the lab
- Field testing: Less common but available
- Correlation: Can sometimes estimate CBR from soil classification

**Practical Application**: Let's say you're bidding a parking lot. The soils report shows CBR = 4%. That's very weak. You know immediately:
- Standard 4" asphalt over 6" base won't be adequate
- You'll need significantly more structural section
- Or you'll need to improve the subgrade (stabilization, excavation, etc.)
- Your bid needs to account for this

This is where understanding design helps you avoid underbidding. A competitor who doesn't read the soils report might bid based on "typical" sections and then lose money when the inadequate pavement fails and requires costly repairs.

#### Plasticity Index and Liquid Limit

These tests characterize fine-grained soils (silts and clays) and tell us how they'll behave.

**Liquid Limit (LL)**: The moisture content at which soil changes from plastic to liquid consistency. High liquid limit indicates soil can hold a lot of water.

**Plastic Limit (PL)**: The moisture content at which soil changes from semi-solid to plastic consistency.

**Plasticity Index (PI)**: PI = LL - PL. It measures the range of moisture contents over which soil behaves plastically.

**What These Numbers Mean**:
- **Low PI (< 10)**: Low plasticity—soil is less affected by moisture changes—good
- **Medium PI (10-20)**: Medium plasticity—moisture affects behavior—fair
- **High PI (> 20)**: High plasticity—very sensitive to moisture—poor

**Why This Matters**:
- High PI soils expand when wet and shrink when dry
- This creates volume changes that crack pavements
- High PI soils lose strength dramatically when wet
- These soils are problematic and need special treatment

**Identification in the Field**:
- **Low plasticity soil**: Doesn't stick to your boots, crumbles easily
- **High plasticity clay**: Sticky, slick when wet, hard when dry, difficult to work

**Business Application**: If you encounter high PI clay on a project (PI > 20), you should:
- Recommend lime or cement stabilization of subgrade
- Recommend increased drainage attention
- Consider geotextile separation layers
- Increase pavement thickness
- Document conditions carefully
- Get engineer approval before proceeding

These soils are the source of many pavement failures. Taking the extra time to deal with them properly saves expensive repairs later.

#### Subgrade Preparation and Stabilization

Preparing the subgrade properly is critical—it's your foundation.

**Subgrade Preparation Steps**:

1. **Clearing and Stripping**: Remove vegetation, topsoil, organic material, roots
2. **Rough Grading**: Establish approximate elevations
3. **Proof Rolling**: Drive a heavy roller over the subgrade to identify soft spots
4. **Remediation**: Fix any soft spots found during proof rolling
5. **Fine Grading**: Establish final subgrade elevations
6. **Compaction**: Compact subgrade to specified density (typically 95% Standard Proctor)
7. **Acceptance Testing**: Verify density and elevations meet specifications

**Proof Rolling**: This is critical and often skipped. A loaded tandem-axle dump truck or heavy roller is driven over the prepared subgrade. Areas that deflect excessively under the wheel load are soft spots that need remediation.

**Soft Spot Remediation Options**:
- **Excavate and replace** with good material
- **Stabilize** with lime or cement
- **Install geotextile** and thicken base
- **Add underdrain** if water is the problem

**Subgrade Stabilization**: When you have weak subgrade, several treatment options exist:

**Lime Stabilization**:
- Mix hydrated lime or quicklime into clay soil
- Chemical reaction modifies clay structure
- Reduces plasticity, increases strength
- Typical application: 2-6% lime by dry weight of soil, mixed 6-12" deep
- Good for high-plasticity clays

**Cement Stabilization**:
- Mix Portland cement into soil
- Creates a semi-rigid layer
- Much stronger than lime stabilization
- Typical application: 4-8% cement, mixed 6-12" deep
- Good for a wide range of soils

**Mechanical Stabilization**:
- Blend different soil types to improve properties
- Example: Mix sand into clay to reduce plasticity
- No chemical reaction, just mechanical improvement

**When to Recommend Stabilization**:
- CBR < 5%
- High PI clays (PI > 20)
- Wet, saturated soils
- Organic soils (though these may need excavation)
- When increasing pavement thickness isn't cost-effective

**Cost Consideration**: Subgrade stabilization costs money, but it's usually cheaper than the alternatives:
- Excavating and replacing 2 feet of bad soil is expensive
- Putting 8" of asphalt over weak soil (instead of 4" over stabilized soil) is expensive
- Dealing with pavement failure due to weak subgrade is very expensive

**Customer Communication**: "Mr. Customer, the soils test shows very weak clay subgrade on the south side of the lot. We can either excavate and replace it with good material, or we can stabilize it with lime treatment. Stabilization will cost about $X, but it'll give us a stable foundation that will support the pavement for the design life. Without treatment, the pavement will likely fail within a few years, requiring costly repairs."

### Subbase Layer

The subbase sits between the subgrade and the base course. Not every pavement needs a subbase, but in many situations it's beneficial.

#### Materials and Specifications

**Typical Subbase Materials**:
- Granular materials (gravel, crushed stone)
- Select subgrade material (better soil from elsewhere)
- Recycled materials (crushed concrete, RAP)
- Stabilized materials (cement-treated or lime-treated)

**Gradation**: Usually coarser and less strict than base course requirements. The goal is adequate strength and drainage, not maximum density.

**Quality Requirements**: Lower than base course but still significant:
- Some specifications for maximum fines content
- Minimum durability requirements
- Plasticity limits

#### Thickness Requirements

Typical subbase thickness: 4-12 inches, depending on:
- Subgrade strength (weaker subgrade needs thicker subbase)
- Base course thickness (thicker base may eliminate need for subbase)
- Drainage needs
- Frost protection requirements
- Economics (cost of subbase material vs. other solutions)

**When Subbase Is Used**:
- Weak subgrade (CBR < 10%)
- Need for separation between subgrade and base (especially if subgrade is fine-grained)
- Drainage layer to keep base dry
- Frost protection (need sufficient thickness above frost line)
- Structural enhancement without expensive base course material

**When Subbase Can Be Omitted**:
- Strong subgrade (CBR > 20%)
- Well-drained conditions
- No frost concerns
- Cost constraints (sometimes better to put money into thicker asphalt or base)

#### Drainage Functions

Subbase can serve as a drainage layer:

**Drainage Subbase Design**:
- Use open-graded material (minimal fines)
- Provide outlet for water (edge drains, underdrains)
- Use geotextile separation between subgrade and subbase
- Slope the subbase to drain to collection points

**Benefits of Drainage Subbase**:
- Keeps base course dry (maintains strength)
- Removes water that infiltrates through cracks
- Reduces frost heave potential
- Extends pavement life significantly

**Critical Point**: A drainage layer only works if water can get out. You need edge drains or underdrains to collect and remove water. Otherwise, water just sits in the subbase, defeating the purpose.

### Base Course

The base course is the primary structural layer beneath the asphalt. It's your main load-distributing layer and the key to pavement performance.

#### Crushed Aggregate Base (CAB) Specifications

High-quality crushed stone base is the gold standard for flexible pavement support.

**Key Requirements**:

**Aggregate Quality**:
- Durable stone (low abrasion loss)
- Resistant to weathering (soundness)
- Minimal deleterious materials
- Low plasticity (PI < 6 typically)

**Gradation**:
- Dense-graded (particles of all sizes fit together efficiently)
- Specified percentage passing each sieve size
- Enough fines for density, not so many that drainage suffers
- Typically ranges from 1.5" or 2" maximum size down to fines

**Fractured Faces**: Modern specifications typically require:
- 90-100% of coarse aggregate particles have at least one fractured face
- 50-75% have two or more fractured faces
- This ensures angular particles that lock together for stability

**Why These Requirements Matter**: Low-quality base leads to:
- Loss of support under the asphalt
- Pumping (base material being ejected through cracks)
- Rutting and deformation
- Premature pavement failure

#### Gradation Requirements

A typical dense-graded base gradation might look like:

| Sieve Size | Percent Passing |
|------------|-----------------|
| 1.5" | 100% |
| 1" | 80-100% |
| 3/4" | 70-92% |
| 3/8" | 50-70% |
| No. 4 | 35-55% |
| No. 40 | 15-30% |
| No. 200 | 5-15% |

**Why Gradation Matters**:
- Proper gradation ensures particles fit together efficiently
- Too many fines → material acts like soil (weak, moisture-sensitive)
- Too few fines → material doesn't compact well (low density, instability)
- Gap-graded base (missing certain sizes) doesn't compact or stabilize properly

**Checking Gradation**: Base producers should provide sieve analysis results. If gradation is off-spec, the material won't perform properly. Don't accept questionable base material—it's the foundation of your pavement.

#### Compaction Standards

Compaction is critical for base performance.

**Typical Density Requirements**:
- **95-100% of Standard Proctor density** (AASHTO T-99)
- Or **95-98% of Modified Proctor density** (AASHTO T-180)

Standard Proctor is more common for base courses. Modified Proctor is more stringent (used for heavy-duty applications).

**Testing Methods**:
- Nuclear density gauge (most common for field testing)
- Sand cone test
- Drive cylinder test
- Lab testing of field samples

**Compaction Procedures**:
- Place base in lifts (typically 6-8" maximum loose thickness)
- Moisture condition the material (usually 4-6% moisture for optimal compaction)
- Compact with appropriate equipment (vibratory roller, pneumatic roller)
- Test density and adjust as needed
- Repeat for additional lifts if required

**Why Compaction Matters**: Poorly compacted base:
- Consolidates further under traffic (causing rutting)
- Has low strength
- Allows excessive deflection
- Shortens pavement life dramatically

**Business Reality**: Compaction takes time, and time costs money. Some contractors are tempted to rush compaction to keep moving. Don't. Inadequate base compaction is one of the top causes of premature pavement failure, and failures lead to costly repairs, damaged reputation, and lost future business.

**Quality Control**: 
- Test density frequently (at least every 500-1000 square yards)
- Document all test results
- Don't pave over uncompacted or poorly compacted base
- If density tests fail, add more compaction effort or adjust moisture

#### Alternative Base Materials

Besides virgin crushed stone, several alternative base materials are available:

**Recycled Concrete Aggregate (RCA)**:
- Crushed concrete from demolition
- Can perform as well as virgin stone if properly processed
- Usually costs less than virgin aggregate
- Environmental benefits (reuse, landfill diversion)
- May need testing to verify quality
- Some specifications have percentage limits on RCA use

**Reclaimed Asphalt Pavement (RAP)**:
- Milled-up old asphalt pavement
- Can be used as base material (unbound RAP)
- Provides some binding from residual asphalt
- Usually cheaper than virgin aggregate
- Good drainage properties
- Some specifications limit RAP in base course

**Cement-Stabilized Base**:
- Aggregate base mixed with Portland cement
- Creates a semi-rigid base layer
- Much higher strength than unstabilized base
- Can use lower-quality aggregate since cement provides strength
- More expensive but can reduce overall pavement thickness
- Requires careful construction (timing, curing, joints)

**Lime-Stabilized Base**:
- Aggregate base or native material mixed with lime
- Improves plasticity and strength
- Good for areas with marginal base materials
- Less expensive than cement stabilization but also less strength improvement

**When to Use Alternative Materials**:
- Cost savings on large projects
- Sustainability requirements
- Local availability (virgin aggregate scarce or expensive in some areas)
- Approved by specifications
- Proper quality control in place

**When to Stick with Virgin Aggregate**:
- High-value projects where performance is critical
- Specifications don't allow alternatives
- Recycled materials not available locally at competitive cost
- Uncertainty about recycled material quality

### Asphalt Layers

Modern pavement design often uses multiple asphalt layers, each optimized for its function.

#### Binder Course

**Function**: Provide the majority of the structural strength in the asphalt portion of the pavement.

**Characteristics**:
- Coarser gradation than surface mix (typically 3/4" or 1" NMAS)
- Higher stability emphasis
- Less concern about surface appearance
- May use lower PG grade binder (doesn't see the temperature extremes of surface)
- Often less expensive per ton than surface mix

**Typical Thickness**: 2-4 inches per lift

**Placement**: Usually placed directly on base course, below the surface course

**When Used**: Multi-lift construction where total asphalt thickness exceeds about 4 inches

**Business Advantage**: Using a binder course lets you optimize costs. Instead of 6 inches of expensive surface mix, you might use 4 inches of less expensive binder mix plus 2 inches of surface mix. Same or better structural capacity, lower cost.

#### Intermediate Course

**Function**: Transitional layer between binder course and surface course (used in thick pavements with 3+ asphalt lifts).

**Characteristics**:
- Gradation between binder and surface (typically 1/2" NMAS)
- Moderate binder content
- Transitional properties

**When Used**: 
- Very thick pavements (8+ inches total asphalt)
- Major highways
- Airport pavements
- Heavy-duty industrial applications

**Typical Projects**: Most commercial and residential work doesn't need an intermediate course—you'll typically use just surface and binder, or just a single surface course.

#### Surface Course

**Function**: Provide smooth ride, appearance, waterproofing, and friction for safety.

**Characteristics**:
- Fine gradation (typically 3/8" or 1/2" NMAS)
- High binder content (for durability and waterproofing)
- Highest quality aggregate (for appearance and friction)
- Highest PG grade binder (experiences temperature extremes)
- Smooth finish
- Appropriate texture for friction

**Typical Thickness**: 1.5-2.5 inches

**Critical Requirements**:
- Proper density (typically 93-96% of theoretical maximum)
- Good ride quality (smoothness)
- Adequate surface drainage (no ponding)
- Proper slope (minimum 1-2%)

**Why Surface Course Quality Matters**: This is what your customer sees and drives on. A rough, uneven surface reflects poorly on your workmanship, even if the structural design is perfect. Moreover, the surface course is the water barrier—poor surface density lets water in, accelerating pavement deterioration.

**Business Application**: Don't skimp on surface course quality to save a few dollars. The surface is your showcase. A smooth, attractive, well-constructed surface course leads to referrals and repeat business. A rough, poorly finished surface damages your reputation.

## 3.3 Thickness Design

Now we get into the meat of pavement design: how thick do the layers need to be?

### Structural Number (SN) Concept

The Structural Number is a key concept in AASHTO pavement design. Think of it as a "strength score" for your pavement structure.

#### Formula: SN = a₁D₁ + a₂D₂M₂ + a₃D₃M₃

Let's break this down:

**SN** = Structural Number (unitless number representing pavement strength)

**a₁, a₂, a₃** = Layer coefficients (strength per inch of each material)
- a₁ = surface course layer coefficient
- a₂ = base course layer coefficient  
- a₃ = subbase layer coefficient

**D₁, D₂, D₃** = Layer thicknesses in inches
- D₁ = surface course thickness
- D₂ = base course thickness
- D₃ = subbase thickness

**M₂, M₃** = Drainage coefficients (adjustment for drainage quality)
- M₂ = drainage coefficient for base
- M₃ = drainage coefficient for subbase
- (Note: No M₁ for surface course—it's always 1.0)

**The Concept**: Each inch of material contributes to the total structural strength based on that material's layer coefficient. Better materials have higher coefficients (contribute more strength per inch).

#### Layer Coefficients (a-values)

Layer coefficients represent the structural contribution per inch of material.

**Typical Values**:
- **Asphalt concrete**: 0.35-0.44 (depends on quality and mix type)
- **Crushed aggregate base**: 0.10-0.14
- **Granular subbase**: 0.08-0.12
- **Cement-stabilized base**: 0.15-0.30
- **Lime-stabilized subgrade**: 0.10-0.15

**What These Numbers Mean**: One inch of asphalt (a = 0.40) contributes about 3-4 times as much structural strength as one inch of aggregate base (a = 0.12).

**Implications for Design**:
- Asphalt is structurally efficient but expensive
- Base is less efficient structurally but cheaper
- The trick is finding the optimum balance

**Example Calculation**:
Let's say you have:
- 4" asphalt surface (a₁ = 0.40)
- 8" crushed aggregate base (a₂ = 0.12, M₂ = 1.0)
- No subbase

SN = (0.40 × 4) + (0.12 × 8 × 1.0) + 0
SN = 1.60 + 0.96 + 0
SN = 2.56

If design traffic and subgrade require SN = 3.0, this section is inadequate. You'd need to increase thickness somewhere.

#### Drainage Coefficients (M-values)

Drainage coefficients adjust the structural contribution of base and subbase layers based on how quickly water drains.

**Typical Values**:
- **Excellent drainage** (water removed in <2 hours): M = 1.20-1.40
- **Good drainage** (water removed in 1 day): M = 1.00-1.20
- **Fair drainage** (water removed in 1 week): M = 0.80-1.00
- **Poor drainage** (water removed in 1 month): M = 0.60-0.80
- **Very poor drainage** (water remains): M = 0.40-0.60

**What This Means**: Good drainage makes base materials perform better, while poor drainage reduces their effectiveness. Water-saturated base has much less strength than dry base.

**Business Application**: This is why drainage is so important and why you should always recommend proper drainage design. A well-drained pavement with 6" of base might perform as well as a poorly drained pavement with 8" of base. Spending money on drainage systems (underdrains, proper slopes, edge drains) is an investment in pavement longevity.

#### Thickness Determination

The design process works like this:

1. **Determine required SN** from design charts or equations (based on traffic, subgrade strength, reliability, serviceability loss)
2. **Select layer coefficients** for materials you'll use
3. **Select drainage coefficients** based on drainage design
4. **Calculate layer thicknesses** that provide required SN

**Constraints**:
- Minimum asphalt thickness (typically 3-4" depending on traffic)
- Minimum base thickness (if used, typically 4-6")
- Construction considerations (can you compact a 2" lift effectively?)
- Cost optimization (what combination costs least while meeting SN requirement?)

**Example Design Problem**:

Given:
- Required SN = 3.5
- Subgrade CBR = 6% (poor soil)
- Will use asphalt surface (a = 0.40), aggregate base (a = 0.12), good drainage (M = 1.0)
- Want minimum asphalt to control cost

Try 4" asphalt:
- SN from asphalt = 0.40 × 4 = 1.60
- Remaining SN needed from base = 3.5 - 1.60 = 1.90
- Base thickness needed = 1.90 / (0.12 × 1.0) = 15.8", say 16"

So one solution: 4" asphalt over 16" base

Try 5" asphalt:
- SN from asphalt = 0.40 × 5 = 2.00
- Remaining SN needed from base = 3.5 - 2.00 = 1.50
- Base thickness needed = 1.50 / (0.12 × 1.0) = 12.5", say 13"

Alternative solution: 5" asphalt over 13" base

**Which is better?** Compare costs:
- Option 1: 4" asphalt + 16" base
- Option 2: 5" asphalt + 13" base

If asphalt costs $100/ton and base costs $15/ton, you'd calculate the cost per square yard for each option and choose the lower cost (assuming both meet structural requirements).

**Practical Note**: Exact structural design is usually done by engineers, but understanding this process helps you:
- Verify designs are reasonable
- Recognize when soil conditions differ from design assumptions
- Suggest cost-effective alternatives
- Communicate intelligently with engineers

### Traffic Analysis

Traffic is the other major input to pavement design (along with subgrade strength). We need to quantify how much traffic the pavement will carry over its design life.

#### Equivalent Single Axle Load (ESAL) Calculations

Different vehicles do different amounts of damage to pavements. A bicycle does essentially zero damage. A passenger car does minimal damage. An 80,000-pound truck does significant damage. We need a way to convert all these different vehicles into a common unit.

**The ESAL Concept**: All traffic is converted to an equivalent number of 18,000-pound single axle loads (ESALs). This is based on research showing that damage increases exponentially with axle weight (roughly to the 4th power).

**What This Means**:
- One pass of an 18,000-lb single axle = 1.0 ESAL
- One pass of a 10,000-lb single axle ≈ 0.1 ESAL (much less damage)
- One pass of a 36,000-lb tandem axle ≈ 1.5 ESALs
- One pass of a passenger car ≈ 0.0002 ESALs (essentially nothing)

**The 4th Power Law**: This explains why heavy trucks cause so much more damage than cars:
- A 10,000-lb axle does 0.08 times the damage of an 18,000-lb axle (almost nothing)
- An 18,000-lb axle does 1.0 times the damage (by definition)
- A 22,000-lb axle does 2.1 times the damage
- A 30,000-lb axle does 6.8 times the damage
- A 40,000-lb axle does 20.3 times the damage

**Implications**: 
- Passenger cars contribute almost nothing to pavement structural loading
- What matters is heavy trucks
- Overloaded trucks cause disproportionate damage

#### Traffic Volume Projections

To calculate design ESALs, we need to project traffic over the design life.

**Typical Design Process**:

1. **Current Traffic Volume**: Average daily traffic (ADT) or trucks per day
2. **Growth Rate**: Annual traffic increase (typically 2-4%)
3. **Design Period**: How many years (typically 20)
4. **Truck Percentage**: What percent of vehicles are trucks
5. **Axle Load Distribution**: What types and weights of trucks
6. **Directional Distribution**: If two-way road, traffic in each direction
7. **Lane Distribution**: If multi-lane, traffic in each lane

**Example Calculation** (simplified):

A two-lane road has:
- ADT = 5,000 vehicles/day
- 10% trucks
- Growth rate = 3% per year
- Design period = 20 years
- Each truck = 2.0 ESALs on average

Calculation:
- Trucks per day = 5,000 × 0.10 = 500
- ESALs per day = 500 × 2.0 = 1,000
- ESALs per year (current) = 1,000 × 365 = 365,000
- With 3% growth over 20 years, use a growth factor
- Cumulative ESALs ≈ 10 million (using growth factor formula)

Design for the most heavily loaded lane (typically 80-90% of traffic in multi-lane sections).

**Traffic Categories** (simplified):
- **Low Volume**: < 50,000 ESALs over design life
  - Residential driveways and streets
  - Parking lots with minimal truck traffic
  - Bike paths

- **Medium Volume**: 50,000-500,000 ESALs
  - Residential collectors
  - Light commercial parking
  - Low-traffic municipal streets

- **Medium-High Volume**: 500,000-2 million ESALs
  - Commercial arterials
  - Shopping center access
  - Moderate commercial parking

- **High Volume**: 2-10 million ESALs
  - Major highways
  - Heavy commercial areas
  - Truck routes

- **Very High Volume**: > 10 million ESALs
  - Interstate highways
  - Major urban arterials
  - Ports and heavy industrial

#### Heavy Vehicle Considerations

In most cases, design traffic is dominated by heavy trucks.

**Key Truck Types**:
- **Single-unit trucks** (delivery trucks, dump trucks): 2-3 ESALs per pass typically
- **Tractor-trailers** (18-wheelers): 1-3 ESALs per pass depending on loading
- **Overloaded trucks**: Can be 5-10+ ESALs per pass

**Special Considerations**:
- **Trash trucks**: Very heavy, frequent stop-and-start, damaging
- **Concrete trucks**: Very heavy when loaded, high tire pressures
- **Buses**: Moderate weight but very frequent loading (bus routes need stronger design)
- **Delivery trucks**: Increasingly important with e-commerce growth

**Standing Loads**: Vehicles that stop or move very slowly cause more pavement damage than the same vehicle moving at highway speed:
- Traffic signals (cars stopping and accelerating)
- Bus stops
- Toll booths
- Truck parking areas
- Loading docks

For these areas, consider:
- Increasing pavement thickness
- Using higher-grade binder (PG 70 instead of PG 64)
- Using SMA or other rut-resistant mix
- Thickening the pavement in stop/start zones

#### Loading Conditions and Stress Concentrations

Beyond just counting ESALs, we need to consider how vehicles load the pavement.

**Tire Pressure Effects**:
- Highway trucks: 90-120 psi typical
- Passenger cars: 30-35 psi typical
- Aircraft: 150-200+ psi

Higher tire pressures create higher stresses in the pavement structure. This is one reason airport pavements need special design.

**Channelized Traffic**: When all traffic follows the same wheel path (narrow lanes, guided busways, loading areas with defined paths), damage is concentrated. May need:
- Thicker pavement in wheel paths
- Full-width thickening
- More frequent maintenance

**Turning Movements**: High horizontal shear stresses:
- Intersections
- Roundabouts  
- Parking lot aisles
- Loading dock approaches

These areas may need:
- Thicker pavement
- Stiffer binder (polymer-modified)
- SMA or other high-stability mix

**Environmental Loading**: Don't forget that temperature and moisture also "load" the pavement:
- Thermal stress (expansion/contraction)
- Freeze-thaw cycles
- Wet-dry cycles

### Design Considerations

Beyond the mechanics of calculating structural numbers and ESALs, several practical considerations affect pavement design.

#### Full-Depth Asphalt vs. Aggregate Base Designs

**Full-Depth Asphalt**: All asphalt pavement directly on subgrade (no aggregate base).

**Advantages**:
- Eliminates problems with contaminated or poorly compacted base
- Faster construction (no base layer to place and compact)
- Good for areas with poor drainage or high water table
- Can be more economical where aggregate is expensive
- Less susceptible to pumping and base erosion

**Disadvantages**:
- Usually requires more asphalt (more expensive per square yard)
- Not always more economical
- May have thermal cracking issues in cold climates

**When to Use Full-Depth**:
- Poor drainage conditions
- Weak, fine-grained subgrade (high plasticity clay)
- Aggregate is expensive or unavailable
- Fast-track construction
- Areas where base quality control is difficult

**Conventional Asphalt Over Aggregate Base**: This is the most common approach.

**Advantages**:
- Usually most economical
- Uses materials efficiently (expensive asphalt where loads are highest, cheaper base below)
- Base provides drainage layer
- Traditional, well-understood

**Disadvantages**:
- More construction steps
- Base quality control is critical
- Potential for base contamination or poor compaction

**When to Use Conventional Design**:
- Most situations (it's the standard for good reason)
- Good drainage available
- Quality aggregate locally available
- Normal construction schedules

**Business Decision**: For most commercial and municipal work, conventional design (asphalt over aggregate base) is your go-to. Consider full-depth when site conditions or economics favor it, but verify with design engineer.

#### Minimum Lift Thickness Requirements

You can't place asphalt in arbitrarily thin layers and expect good results.

**The 3-4× NMAS Rule**: Minimum compacted lift thickness should be at least 3-4 times the nominal maximum aggregate size (NMAS).

**Examples**:
- 3/4" NMAS mix: Minimum 2.25-3" thickness
- 1/2" NMAS mix: Minimum 1.5-2" thickness  
- 3/8" NMAS mix: Minimum 1.1-1.5" thickness

**Why This Matters**:
- Thin lifts are difficult to compact properly
- Large aggregate particles can't orient properly in thin lifts
- Segregation problems increase in thin lifts
- Density achievement is more difficult

**Practical Implications**:
- If you're placing 2" lifts, use 1/2" NMAS mix maximum
- If you're placing 3" lifts, you can use 3/4" NMAS mix
- If specification calls for 1.5" surface course, it must be fine-graded (3/8" NMAS)

**Multiple Lift Construction**: For thick asphalt sections, place in multiple lifts:
- Each lift typically 1.5-3 inches compacted thickness
- Thicker lifts retain heat longer (good for compaction)
- Very thick lifts can be difficult to compact at depth
- Tack coat between lifts (though fresh-on-fresh can bond without tack)

**Business Application**: If plans show 6" of asphalt in a single lift using 3/4" mix, that's a problem. You can't compact 6" in one pass effectively. Recommend two 3" lifts instead. If the engineer insists on one lift, get it in writing that you're following their direction—don't take responsibility for density that you can't achieve.

## 3.4 Climate and Environmental Design Factors

Pavement design isn't just about carrying loads. The environment—temperature and moisture—affects pavement performance as much as traffic does.

### Temperature Effects

#### PG Binder Selection for Climate

We covered the Performance Grade (PG) system in Chapter 1, but let's review why it matters for design.

**Climate-Based Selection**: The PG system matches binder to climate:
- **First number** (PG 64): Maximum 7-day average high pavement temperature
- **Second number** (-22): Minimum pavement temperature

**Examples**:
- **Phoenix**: PG 76-10 (very hot summers, mild winters)
- **Minneapolis**: PG 58-34 (moderate summers, very cold winters)
- **Mid-Atlantic (Virginia)**: PG 64-22 (moderate summers and winters)

**Getting It Wrong**:
- Use PG 64-22 in Phoenix → rutting failure in first summer
- Use PG 76-10 in Minneapolis → thermal cracking in first winter

**Traffic Adjustments**: Heavy or slow traffic generates more heat in pavement:
- May need to bump up one grade for heavy traffic
- Example: PG 64-22 → PG 70-22 for heavy truck route

**Your Responsibility**: If you're provided with a pavement design, verify the PG grade is appropriate for your location. If plans show PG 64-22 for a project in southern Texas, question it—it's probably an error. Using wrong binder grade is a recipe for failure.

#### Thermal Stress and Cracking

Asphalt pavement expands when hot and contracts when cold.

**Thermal Cracking**: When pavement cools, it tries to contract. If contraction is restrained (by friction with base, or by surrounding pavement), tensile stress develops. If stress exceeds the tensile strength of the mix, the pavement cracks.

**Where Thermal Cracking Occurs**:
- Cold climates (significant temperature drops)
- When low-temperature PG grade is insufficient
- Long, straight sections (most restraint)
- Over joints in underlying pavement

**Preventing Thermal Cracking**:
- Select appropriate low-temperature PG grade
- Use polymer-modified binders (more flexibility)
- Proper mix design (avoid overly stiff mixes)
- Minimize restraint (proper joint construction)

**Business Consideration**: In very cold climates, make sure specifications call for appropriate low-temperature binder grade. Using PG 64-22 in Minnesota (where PG 58-34 is needed) will lead to cracking and warranty claims.

#### Freeze-Thaw Considerations

In climates with freeze-thaw cycles, water is your enemy.

**The Freeze-Thaw Problem**:
1. Water infiltrates pavement through cracks
2. Water gets into base and subgrade
3. Water freezes and expands (9% volume increase)
4. Expansion creates pressure, cracks pavement
5. Ice melts, leaving void
6. Repeated cycles cause progressive damage

**Frost Heave**: Water freezing in subgrade creates ice lenses that lift the pavement, causing:
- Cracking and breaking
- Uneven surface
- Loss of support when ice melts

**Design Responses**:
- Adequate pavement thickness above frost line
- Good drainage (remove water before it can freeze)
- Non-frost-susceptible base materials
- Properly selected asphalt binder
- Crack sealing to prevent water infiltration

**Frost Depth**: How deep frost penetrates varies by location:
- Northern states: 4-6 feet typical
- Mid-Atlantic: 2-3 feet
- Southern states: little to no frost

Pavement structure should extend below frost depth, or use non-frost-susceptible materials (coarse-grained, free-draining materials don't heave).

### Moisture Management

Water is the single biggest enemy of pavement durability. Good design prevents water from getting into and staying in the pavement structure.

#### Drainage Design Requirements

**Surface Drainage**: 
- Minimum slope: 1-2% (1.5% typical for parking lots, 2% preferred for roads)
- Some say 1/4" per foot (2%) minimum
- No ponding or standing water
- Water directed to inlets or edges

**Subsurface Drainage**:
- If subgrade or base are saturated, strength is dramatically reduced
- Need to remove infiltrated water
- Need to control groundwater

**Drainage Systems**:
- **Inlets and storm sewers**: Collect surface water
- **Underdrains**: Perforated pipes in base to collect and remove water
- **Edge drains**: Collect water from pavement edges
- **Daylighting**: Direct drainage to outlet (ditch, inlet, etc.)

#### Minimum Slopes (Typically 2% or 1/4 inch per foot)

**Why Slope Matters**:
- Standing water accelerates pavement aging
- Water infiltrates through surface cracks
- Ponding is an aesthetic negative
- Ice formation in winter
- Hydroplaning risk

**Achieving Proper Slope**:
- Design with adequate slope from the start
- Maintain slope during construction (don't let low spots develop)
- Crown roads (high in center, slope to both sides)
- Slope parking lots to drainage inlets

**Common Problem**: Pavement settles over time, creating low spots and ponding. This is why:
- Proper subgrade and base compaction is critical
- Drainage design should include safety factor
- Consider future settlement in design

**Business Application**: When you see standing water on a pavement, it's almost always a design or construction deficiency:
- Inadequate slope in design
- Poor grade control during construction
- Settlement of subgrade or base
- Clogged drainage

Don't accept plans with inadequate slope. Explain to the customer that proper drainage is essential for pavement life. A few extra dollars to improve grading and add inlets pays off in longer pavement life.

#### Subsurface Drainage Systems

For good performance, you need to remove water that gets into the pavement structure.

**Underdrains**: Perforated pipe in a trench at the bottom of the base course:
- Typically 4-6" perforated PVC or corrugated HDPE
- Wrapped in geotextile filter fabric
- Bedded in coarse aggregate
- Outlets to daylight or storm system
- Spaced 20-50 feet apart depending on conditions

**Edge Drains**: Similar to underdrains but at pavement edges:
- Intercept water before it enters pavement structure
- Collect water draining laterally from base course

**When to Use Subsurface Drainage**:
- High water table
- Poor subgrade drainage
- Heavy rainfall areas
- Freeze-thaw climates
- Long pavement life desired
- Experience with drainage problems in area

**Cost-Benefit**: Underdrains cost money (materials, labor, trenching). But consider:
- Properly drained pavement can last 2-3 times longer than poorly drained pavement
- The cost of underdrains is typically 5-10% of total project cost
- Avoiding premature failure saves much more than underdrains cost

**Recommendation**: For any significant project (>$50K), include subsurface drainage if conditions warrant. Explain to customer that it's cheap insurance for pavement longevity.

#### Moisture Sensitivity of Materials

Some materials lose strength dramatically when wet.

**Moisture-Susceptible Materials**:
- Fine-grained soils (silts, clays)
- Some aggregates (soft sandstones, shales)
- Asphalt mixes with inadequate anti-stripping additives

**Testing for Moisture Susceptibility**:
- **TSR (Tensile Strength Ratio)**: Compares strength of dry vs. water-soaked samples
- TSR > 0.80 is generally considered acceptable
- TSR < 0.70 indicates moisture damage potential

**Preventing Moisture Damage**:
- Use quality aggregates
- Add anti-stripping additives (liquid or hydrated lime)
- Achieve proper density (less air voids = less water infiltration)
- Seal surface cracks promptly
- Maintain good drainage

**Business Consideration**: If you're using aggregates known to be moisture-susceptible, insist on anti-stripping additives in the mix. This is cheap insurance. Better yet, avoid questionable aggregates if possible.

## 3.5 Application-Specific Design

Different applications have different requirements. Let's look at how design varies by application type.

### 3.5.1 Commercial Asphalt Pavement Design

#### Parking Lots

Parking lots are the bread-and-butter of many asphalt contractors. Understanding proper design helps you deliver quality and explain value.

**Traffic Volume and Loading Analysis**:
- Light use: Small office, medical office → few vehicles per day
- Medium use: Shopping center, restaurant → moderate daily traffic
- Heavy use: Shopping mall, grocery store, big-box retail → high daily traffic
- Very heavy use: Distribution center, trucking terminal → heavy trucks, frequent loading

**Pavement Thickness: Typically 4-6 Inches**:
- **2-3 inches asphalt**: Very light use, car-only parking (but this is minimal—4" is more typical minimum)
- **4 inches asphalt**: Standard light commercial parking (retail, office)
- **5 inches asphalt**: Moderate commercial parking with some truck traffic
- **6 inches asphalt**: Heavy commercial parking with regular truck traffic
- **6-8+ inches asphalt**: Distribution centers, truck terminals, loading areas

**Base Course**:
- Typically 4-8 inches of compacted aggregate base
- More base for weaker subgrade
- Less base for stronger subgrade
- For truck areas, 8-12 inches common

**Typical Section** (most commercial parking):
- 4" asphalt (2" surface + 2" binder, or single lift)
- 6" aggregate base
- Prepared subgrade
- Good drainage

**Layout and Circulation Patterns**:
- Adequate aisle widths (24-26' for two-way, 18-20' for one-way with 90° parking)
- Proper turning radii (especially for trucks)
- Fire lanes (required width and weight capacity)
- ADA-compliant accessible routes and parking
- Traffic flow that minimizes conflicts

**Drainage Provisions and Slope Requirements**:
- Minimum 1.5-2% slope
- Slope to drainage inlets or edges
- Inlets sized for rainfall intensity
- No ponding water
- Consider detention/retention requirements

**ADA Compliance Requirements**:
- Accessible parking spaces (number based on total spaces)
- Van-accessible spaces (wider with access aisle)
- Maximum 2% slope in accessible spaces and routes
- Proper striping and signage
- Curb ramps where required
- Detectable warnings at certain locations

**Business Opportunity**: Understanding ADA requirements lets you catch design deficiencies and recommend corrections. This demonstrates professionalism and protects your customer from liability. Offer to review plans for ADA compliance—many small projects don't get thorough review, and catching problems before construction saves expensive fixes later.

**Cost Factors**:
- Size (larger projects have lower unit cost)
- Site preparation needs (clearing, grading)
- Subgrade conditions
- Drainage requirements
- Striping and signing
- Curbs, inlets, lighting, landscaping

**Winning Commercial Parking Lot Bids**: Focus on value, not just price:
- Emphasize proper design for expected traffic
- Include maintenance recommendations
- Offer warranty
- Show past projects (photos, references)
- Highlight quality control procedures
- Offer maintenance contracts

#### Loading Docks and Heavy-Use Areas

Loading docks and areas with concentrated heavy loads need special design attention.

**Increased Structural Requirements**:
- Heavier trucks (full tractor-trailers, up to 80,000 lbs gross)
- Slower speeds (more pavement stress)
- Standing loads (trucks parked while loading/unloading)
- Turning movements (high shear stress)

**Typical Design**:
- 6-8 inches asphalt (often 4" surface + 4" binder, or 3"+3"+2")
- 8-12 inches aggregate base
- Consider full-depth asphalt in some cases
- Higher PG grade binder (PG 70 instead of PG 64)
- Consider SMA or polymer-modified mix

**Heavy Vehicle Considerations**:
- Dumpster pads: Very heavy when full, often need 8-10" asphalt
- Truck scales: Heavy static loads, need 8-12" asphalt plus reinforced base
- Container handling equipment: Extremely heavy, need engineering design
- Frequent truck traffic: Accelerated pavement aging, plan for shorter life or stronger initial design

**Turning and Braking Zones**:
- Approaches to docks: Heavy braking, need extra thickness or high-stability mix
- Turning areas: High shear stress, consider SMA or thickened sections
- Backup areas: Slower movements, standing loads, need robust design

**Common Mistakes**:
- Using standard parking lot design for loading dock area (will rut and fail)
- Not accounting for concentrated loads from outriggers or stands
- Inadequate drainage (fuel and fluid spills create problems)

**Your Value Proposition**: When bidding loading dock work, explain that this isn't standard parking lot. Show them the design differences and why the extra cost is justified. A rutted loading dock shuts down operations—that's expensive. Proper design is cheap by comparison.

### 3.5.2 Residential Asphalt Pavement Design

#### Driveways

Residential driveways are typically the lightest-duty asphalt pavement application.

**Thickness: Typically 2-3 Inches**:
- **2 inches**: Absolute minimum, only for very light use and good subgrade
- **2.5 inches**: More common minimum, reasonable compromise
- **3 inches**: Preferred thickness, better durability, small cost increase over 2"
- **4+ inches**: For heavy vehicles (RVs, boats, work trucks), or weak subgrade

**Light Vehicle Loading**: Passenger cars, SUVs, pickup trucks:
- 3,000-6,000 pounds typical vehicle weight
- Minimal pavement stress
- No repetitive heavy loading
- Life expectancy 15-20 years with 2-3" asphalt

**Base Requirements (4-6 Inch Compacted Base)**:
- **4 inches**: Minimum, only for excellent subgrade
- **6 inches**: Standard, works for most conditions
- **8+ inches**: Poor subgrade, or customer wants extra durability

**Base Material**: Compacted crushed stone (dense-graded) or processed gravel
- Must be properly compacted (95%+ Standard Proctor)
- Proper gradation
- Not just dumped and graded—must be compacted!

**Aesthetic Considerations**:
- Smooth finish (customers see this every day)
- Proper transition to garage floor (no "speed bump")
- Clean edges
- Proper slope away from house (drainage)
- Consider sealer for appearance (optional, discuss with customer)

**Common Residential Driveway Issues**:
- **Too thin**: Owner wants 2" to save money, but 3" is only incrementally more expensive and much more durable
- **Poor base**: Contractor skips or skimps on base, asphalt fails quickly
- **No compaction**: Base is dumped and graded but not compacted, settles under asphalt
- **Poor drainage**: Water drains toward house or pools on driveway

**Selling Value**: Homeowners often get multiple bids and choose lowest price. Differentiate yourself:
- Explain the difference between 2" and 3" thickness (durability, longevity)
- Show them your base preparation process
- Demonstrate compaction equipment
- Offer warranty
- Show photos of past driveways
- Offer seal coating maintenance plan

**Pricing Reality**: A typical residential driveway might be 600-800 sq ft. At $8-12 per square foot (depending on market and specifics), that's $4,800-$9,600. For many homeowners, this is a significant expense. They're price-sensitive and may not understand quality differences. Your job is to educate them so they see value, not just price.

#### Private Roads and Subdivisions

**Collector and Local Street Design**:
- Light traffic (residents and visitors only)
- Mostly passenger cars
- Typically 3-4" asphalt over 6-8" base
- 20-25 foot width typical for two-way traffic
- Narrower for one-way or low-volume

**Lower Traffic Volumes**: 
- Few hundred vehicles per day typical
- Almost no heavy trucks (delivery, trash, occasional moving van)
- Design life 15-20 years

**Cost-Effective Solutions**:
- Use local standard sections
- Minimize grading and drainage work
- Stage construction if possible (base one year, surface next year when houses are built and construction traffic is done)
- Consider chip seal or slurry seal surface on low-volume streets (less expensive than HMA)

**Business Opportunity**: Subdivision work can be lucrative:
- Often multiple phases over several years
- Relationship with developer leads to repeat work
- Can be good winter work (base construction when weather doesn't allow paving)

**Challenges**:
- Heavy construction traffic during building phase (tears up new streets)
- Need for phased construction (base first, then surface after construction complete)
- Warranty issues (is damage from construction traffic or pavement failure?)
- Payment issues with developers (more common than with owner-occupied properties)

**Risk Management**: With subdivision work:
- Get payment terms in writing
- Consider progress payments
- May need to accept damage from construction traffic (price this in)
- Inspect existing conditions before construction traffic starts
- Document everything

### 3.5.3 Municipal and Public Works Design

#### City Streets and Roadways

Municipal street work is different from commercial work. You're dealing with public agencies, formal bid processes, and higher standards.

**Functional Classification Systems**:
- **Local streets**: Lowest traffic, provide access to properties
- **Collectors**: Moderate traffic, connect local streets to arterials
- **Arterials**: High traffic, move traffic across town
- **Highways**: Highest traffic, connect cities

Each classification has different design standards (width, thickness, materials, etc.).

**Public Safety Requirements**:
- Adequate sight distance
- Proper curve radii
- Appropriate super-elevation
- Proper drainage
- Traffic calming features (where appropriate)
- ADA-compliant pedestrian facilities

**Utility Coordination**:
- Water, sewer, gas, electric, telecom all under streets
- Need to coordinate construction with utility work
- May need to adjust manholes, valve boxes, etc.
- Avoid paving over utilities that need near-term replacement

**Traffic Management During Construction**:
- Maintain access for residents
- Emergency vehicle access always required
- Traffic control plans required
- Work zone safety (MUTCD compliance)
- Public notification
- Noise and hour restrictions

**Bidding Municipal Work**:
- Formal bid process (sealed bids, public opening)
- Lowest responsible bidder typically wins
- Bonding usually required (bid bond, performance bond, payment bond)
- Must meet prevailing wage requirements (Davis-Bacon)
- Certified payroll documentation
- More paperwork and documentation than private work

**Advantages of Municipal Work**:
- Steady stream of projects
- Payment is reliable (public agencies pay their bills)
- Builds relationships for future work
- Good reputation from public work helps private business

**Challenges**:
- Lower margins (competitive bidding)
- More oversight and inspections
- Stricter specifications
- More paperwork
- Traffic control requirements
- Political considerations

**Getting Started in Municipal Work**:
- Pre-qualify with local agencies
- Start small (small projects to build track record)
- Partner with established contractors as subcontractor
- Develop relationships with public works staff
- Attend pre-bid meetings
- Learn agency standards and procedures

#### Bike Paths and Multi-Use Trails

This is a growing market as communities invest in active transportation and recreation.

**Reduced Structural Requirements**:
- Bikes and pedestrians only (no vehicle loads)
- Very thin pavement adequate (2-3 inches total)
- Lighter base requirements
- Lower traffic volumes

**Typical Design**:
- 2-3 inches asphalt (often single lift of fine-graded mix)
- 4-6 inches aggregate base
- Can sometimes use recycled materials (RAP base, etc.)

**Smooth Surface Specifications**:
- Very smooth surface required (bike safety)
- No bumps or lips at joints
- Proper cross-slope for drainage but not too steep (2% max)
- Transitions must be smooth (no abrupt elevation changes)

**Width and Geometric Standards**:
- 8-10 feet typical width for multi-use path (bikes and pedestrians)
- 10-12 feet for high-traffic multi-use paths
- 5-8 feet for bike-only paths
- Proper clear zones on sides
- Adequate curve radii for bike speeds

**Special Considerations**:
- Often through parks or sensitive areas (environmental concerns)
- Minimize tree removal and disturbance
- May need boardwalks or other structures in wetlands
- Aesthetics matter (visible to many people)
- Drainage without conventional curbs and inlets

**Business Opportunity**:
- Growing market
- Often grant-funded (Transportation Alternatives Program, etc.)
- Good for slow periods (smaller projects, less weather-dependent)
- Good community relations (high-visibility projects people appreciate)

**Challenges**:
- Tight spaces (limited equipment access)
- Hand work often required
- Sensitive stakeholders (environmental groups, neighbors)
- Weather-dependent like all paving
- Public works bidding process

### 3.5.4 Industrial and Heavy-Duty Pavement Design

#### Airports and Runways

Airport paving is a specialized market with very stringent requirements.

**FAA Design Standards (AC 150/5320-6)**:
- Advisory Circular 150/5320-6 is the FAA's pavement design manual
- Mandatory for airports receiving federal funding
- Very detailed specifications
- Rigorous quality control

**FAARFIELD Software Requirements**:
- FAARFIELD is the FAA's pavement design software
- Required for all new airport pavement design
- Accounts for aircraft types, wheel configurations, loading, traffic mix
- Produces layer thicknesses and material requirements

**Heavy Aircraft Loading**:
- Boeing 747: 870,000 lbs max gross takeoff weight
- Airbus A380: 1.2 million lbs max gross
- Typical loads: 200,000-800,000 lbs
- Load distributed over multiple wheels, but still creates very high stress

**High Tire Pressures**:
- Commercial aircraft: 150-200+ psi tire pressure
- Fighter jets: can exceed 300 psi
- Much higher than highway truck tires (90-120 psi)
- Creates higher stress in pavement surface

**Surface Friction Requirements**:
- Critical for safety (stopping aircraft)
- Regular friction testing required
- Minimum friction values mandated
- Groove or texture surface for drainage and friction

**Mix Specifications (FAA P-401, P-403)**:
- P-401: Standard HMA for airfield pavements
- P-403: Polymer-modified HMA for high-stress areas
- Very tight gradation tolerances (much tighter than highway specs)
- High-quality aggregate requirements
- Strict volumetric requirements
- Extensive testing

**Typical Airport Pavement Design**:
- 6-12+ inches of asphalt (depending on aircraft and subgrade)
- Thick aggregate base (12-24 inches common)
- Often full-depth asphalt for high-traffic areas
- PG 76-22 or higher common (especially in hot climates)
- Polymer-modified binders often required

**Construction Challenges**:
- Must work around flight schedules (can't close runway during day often)
- Night work common
- Very short closure windows (6-8 hours to pave and reopen)
- Safety clearances (no FOD - Foreign Object Debris)
- Security requirements
- Extremely strict quality control
- Zero tolerance for errors (safety critical)

**Business Considerations**:
- Specialized market (not for beginners)
- Requires specific experience and qualifications
- Bonding requirements high
- Equipment requirements stringent
- Quality control documentation extensive
- But good margins possible
- Repeat work with airport authorities
- Less price competition (qualification-based selection)

**Getting Into Airport Work**:
- Start with general aviation airports (less critical, lower stress)
- Partner with experienced airport contractor
- Pursue FAA training and certifications
- Build track record on small projects
- Develop relationships with airport authorities
- Invest in quality control capabilities
- Be prepared for extensive documentation

#### Ports and Intermodal Facilities

**Container Handling Equipment Loads**:
- Reach stackers: 200,000+ lbs when loaded
- Gantry cranes: 300,000+ lbs on each rail
- Top handlers: 150,000+ lbs
- Rubber-tired gantries: 100,000+ lbs per wheel
- Container chassis: 80,000 lbs (similar to highway trucks but slower, more frequent)

**Very Heavy-Duty Requirements**:
- Concentrated loads (small contact area)
- Slow moving traffic (higher pavement stress)
- Frequent repetitive loading
- Long design life expected (30-40 years often desired)

**Typical Designs**:
- 12-18+ inches of asphalt (often in multiple lifts)
- Very thick aggregate base (18-36 inches)
- May use cement-stabilized base for added strength
- Full-depth asphalt sometimes used
- High PG grade binders (PG 76-22, PG 82-22)
- Polymer-modified or SMA mixes common

**Large-Stone Mixtures**:
- 1.5-2 inch NMAS common
- Provides maximum stone-on-stone contact
- Excellent load distribution
- Resists rutting and shoving
- Typically used for base courses (underlying surface)

**Special Considerations**:
- Frequent heavy loads cause rapid wear
- Fuel and chemical spills common (need resistant materials)
- May need special surface mixes resistant to petroleum
- Smooth surface needed for container handling equipment
- Precise grade control (containers need level surface)

**Business Opportunity**:
- Large projects (ports are big)
- Good margins (specialized work, less competition)
- Long-term relationships (ports are repeat customers)
- But requires significant capability and experience

**Challenges**:
- Work around port operations (can't shut down entire terminal)
- Phased construction common
- Night and weekend work often required
- Must coordinate with port authorities and terminal operators
- Security requirements
- Environmental regulations (stormwater, spills)

## Closing Thoughts on Chapter 3

Pavement design might seem complex, but the fundamentals are straightforward:
1. **Support the loads**: Adequate thickness to carry traffic without overstressing the subgrade
2. **Manage the environment**: Proper binder selection and drainage design to handle climate
3. **Build it right**: Quality materials, proper compaction, good construction practices

Understanding design helps you:
- **Verify designs are appropriate** for the application
- **Recognize when conditions differ** from design assumptions
- **Recommend cost-effective solutions** that meet performance requirements
- **Explain value to customers** beyond just quoting a price per square yard
- **Avoid costly mistakes** that lead to premature failure

You don't need to be a pavement engineer, but you need to understand enough to:
- Read and interpret design plans
- Recognize obvious problems before they become expensive failures
- Communicate credibly with engineers and customers
- Make intelligent recommendations

In Chapter 4, we'll move from design into construction—how to properly prepare the site, construct the base, place the asphalt, and achieve proper density. Design is important, but construction quality determines whether a pavement actually performs as designed.

Good design + poor construction = failure
Good design + good construction = successful pavement

Let's make sure you know how to deliver on the construction side.
