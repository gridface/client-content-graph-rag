### 3.5.2 Residential Asphalt Pavement Design

#### Driveways

Residential driveways are typically the lightest-duty asphalt pavement application.

**Thickness: Typically 2-3 Inches**:
- **2 inches**: Absolute minimum, only for very light use and good subgrade
- **2.5 inches**: More common minimum, reasonable compromise
- **3 inches**: Preferred thickness, better durability, small cost increase over 2"
- **4+ inches**: For heavy vehicles (RVs, boats, work trucks), or weak subgrade

**Light Vehicle Loading**: Passenger cars, SUVs, pickup trucks:
- 3,000-6,000 pounds typical vehicle weight
- Minimal pavement stress
- No repetitive heavy loading
- Life expectancy 15-20 years with 2-3" asphalt

**Base Requirements (4-6 Inch Compacted Base)**:
- **4 inches**: Minimum, only for excellent subgrade
- **6 inches**: Standard, works for most conditions
- **8+ inches**: Poor subgrade, or customer wants extra durability

**Base Material**: Compacted crushed stone (dense-graded) or processed gravel
- Must be properly compacted (95%+ Standard Proctor)
- Proper gradation
- Not just dumped and graded—must be compacted!

**Aesthetic Considerations**:
- Smooth finish (customers see this every day)
- Proper transition to garage floor (no "speed bump")
- Clean edges
- Proper slope away from house (drainage)
- Consider sealer for appearance (optional, discuss with customer)

**Common Residential Driveway Issues**:
- **Too thin**: Owner wants 2" to save money, but 3" is only incrementally more expensive and much more durable
- **Poor base**: Contractor skips or skimps on base, asphalt fails quickly
- **No compaction**: Base is dumped and graded but not compacted, settles under asphalt
- **Poor drainage**: Water drains toward house or pools on driveway

**Selling Value**: Homeowners often get multiple bids and choose lowest price. Differentiate yourself:
- Explain the difference between 2" and 3" thickness (durability, longevity)
- Show them your base preparation process
- Demonstrate compaction equipment
- Offer warranty
- Show photos of past driveways
- Offer seal coating maintenance plan

**Pricing Reality**: A typical residential driveway might be 600-800 sq ft. At $8-12 per square foot (depending on market and specifics), that's $4,800-$9,600. For many homeowners, this is a significant expense. They're price-sensitive and may not understand quality differences. Your job is to educate them so they see value, not just price.

#### Private Roads and Subdivisions

**Collector and Local Street Design**:
- Light traffic (residents and visitors only)
- Mostly passenger cars
- Typically 3-4" asphalt over 6-8" base
- 20-25 foot width typical for two-way traffic
- Narrower for one-way or low-volume

**Lower Traffic Volumes**: 
- Few hundred vehicles per day typical
- Almost no heavy trucks (delivery, trash, occasional moving van)
- Design life 15-20 years

**Cost-Effective Solutions**:
- Use local standard sections
- Minimize grading and drainage work
- Stage construction if possible (base one year, surface next year when houses are built and construction traffic is done)
- Consider chip seal or slurry seal surface on low-volume streets (less expensive than HMA)

**Business Opportunity**: Subdivision work can be lucrative:
- Often multiple phases over several years
- Relationship with developer leads to repeat work
- Can be good winter work (base construction when weather doesn't allow paving)

**Challenges**:
- Heavy construction traffic during building phase (tears up new streets)
- Need for phased construction (base first, then surface after construction complete)
- Warranty issues (is damage from construction traffic or pavement failure?)
- Payment issues with developers (more common than with owner-occupied properties)

**Risk Management**: With subdivision work:
- Get payment terms in writing
- Consider progress payments
- May need to accept damage from construction traffic (price this in)
- Inspect existing conditions before construction traffic starts
- Document everything

# CHAPTER 4: CONSTRUCTION PRACTICES AND PROCEDURES

In the first three chapters, we covered the fundamentals—what asphalt is made of, the different mix types and their applications, and how pavements are designed to carry loads and last for decades. Now we're getting to where the rubber meets the road: actual construction.

This is where theory becomes practice, where plans become pavement, and where your expertise as a contractor really shows. You can have the perfect mix design and the ideal pavement thickness on paper, but if the construction isn't done right, none of it matters. Poor construction practices can turn a well-designed pavement into a failure waiting to happen, while excellent construction can maximize the performance and life of even a modest design.

Here's what this chapter will do for you: it will give you the knowledge and procedures to build pavements the right way—every time. More importantly, it will help you understand why each step matters so you can explain your process to customers, train your crews effectively, troubleshoot problems when they arise, and avoid the costly callbacks and warranty work that can destroy your profit margins.

When you can walk a customer through your construction process—"We'll proof roll the subgrade to verify stability, place and compact 6 inches of crushed aggregate base in two lifts, apply tack coat to ensure bonding, and then place your asphalt in a single 3-inch lift, compacting it to 94% density while it's still above 185°F"—you're not just another paving contractor. You're a professional who understands that quality construction is what separates a 15-year pavement from a 25-year pavement.

Let's make sure you know every step of the construction process and why it matters.

---

## 4.1 Site Preparation and Earthwork

Before you ever see your first truck loaded with asphalt mix, there's a lot of critical work that has to happen. Site preparation and earthwork aren't glamorous—nobody takes photos of a well-compacted subgrade—but this is the foundation that everything else builds on. Screw up the earthwork, and even the best asphalt in the world won't save the project.

### Clearing and Grubbing

#### Vegetation Removal

This is step one: get all the vegetation off the site. Trees, shrubs, grass, weeds—everything has to go. Here's why it's not as simple as it sounds:

**Root Systems**: Those roots you can't see are the problem. Tree roots can extend 2-3 times the tree's canopy diameter. When you cut down a tree but leave the roots, they will decompose over the next several years, creating voids under your pavement. A year or two after paving, you'll get a call about a depression or even a sinkhole where that old oak tree used to be.

**Best Practice**: 
- Remove trees and large shrubs completely, including stump and major roots
- For trees close to the paving area, grind stumps at least 2 feet below proposed subgrade elevation
- Consider root barriers if you must pave near existing trees you're keeping
- On large sites, use a dozer or excavator to clear rather than hand work

**Business Application**: When you're bidding a job, walk the site carefully and note any significant trees or vegetation. If the plans don't clearly show tree removal and disposal, that's a cost you need to capture in your bid. Underbidding because you didn't account for stump grinding and debris hauling will eat your profit margin before you even start paving.

**Tell Your Customer**: "We're removing all vegetation including roots and stumps to at least 2 feet below the pavement. If we don't, decaying roots will create voids that cause settling later. We're building a foundation that will last."

#### Topsoil Stripping

The top layer of soil at most sites—the topsoil—is rich in organic matter. It's great for growing plants but terrible for supporting pavements.

**Why Strip Topsoil?**:
- Organic matter decomposes over time, causing settlement
- Topsoil is typically loose and has low bearing capacity
- Contains root material that will decay
- Often has high moisture content and poor drainage

**Standard Practice**: Remove 4-12 inches of topsoil across the entire area to be paved, plus extending at least 2-3 feet beyond the pavement edges. The actual depth depends on site conditions—some sites have 6 inches of topsoil, others have 18 inches.

**Where Does It Go?**: 
- Stockpile on site for landscaping (best option if customer has use for it)
- Haul off and dispose (factor this cost into your bid)
- Some areas have topsoil markets—you might actually be able to sell it

**Equipment**: Scrapers, dozers, or excavators with trucks. For small residential jobs, skid steers with buckets work fine.

**Testing Tip**: After stripping, proof roll (we'll discuss this below) to verify you got all the unsuitable material. If you see pumping or excessive deflection, you haven't stripped deep enough.

#### Organic Material Handling

Beyond topsoil, you may encounter other organic materials:

**Peat or Muck**: Highly organic soils, often in wet areas. These are completely unsuitable for pavement support and must be removed entirely and replaced with suitable fill material. Peat can compress 20-30% or more under load—that means massive settlement and pavement failure.

**Wood Debris**: Old stumps, buried lumber, construction debris. All must come out.

**Decomposing Materials**: If you're paving over an old site that had vegetation or wood structures, plan for deeper excavation.

**The Cost of Skipping This Step**: I've seen contractors skip proper clearing to save time or money, thinking "It's just a little topsoil." Then six months later, the pavement has settled 3-4 inches in spots where organic material is decomposing underneath. The cost of removing and replacing that section of pavement is 10 times what proper clearing would have cost. Do it right the first time.


### Excavation and Grading

Once the site is cleared, you need to establish the proper grades and elevations. This is where you create the shape and drainage that will keep your pavement working for decades.

#### Cut and Fill Operations

**Cut**: Removing existing soil to reach design subgrade elevation. You cut in high areas.

**Fill**: Adding and compacting soil to raise areas to design subgrade elevation. You fill in low areas.

The goal is to reach the planned subgrade elevation across the entire site. This elevation is determined by:
- Existing adjacent pavements or curbs you need to match
- Drainage requirements (we need positive drainage away from the pavement)
- Building floor elevations
- Underground utilities that need specific cover depths
- Property boundaries and easements

**Critical Numbers**:
- Typically, you want at least 2% slope (1/4 inch per foot) for drainage
- Steeper slopes (up to 5-6%) are fine and drain better
- Absolutely no flat areas or negative slopes (where water ponds)
- Crown roadways so water drains to both sides

**Equipment for Grading**:
- **Motor Graders**: The gold standard for precise grading work. A skilled grader operator can hit elevations to within half an inch.
- **Dozers**: Good for moving large amounts of material, rough grading
- **Excavators**: Versatile for digging, loading trucks, fine work around utilities
- **Scrapers**: Excellent for moving dirt over distances on large sites
- **Skid Steers**: Perfect for small sites, tight spaces, residential work

**Grade Control Methods**:
- String lines with offset stakes
- Laser level systems on graders
- GPS machine control systems (expensive but very accurate)
- Traditional grade stakes
- Benchmark elevations tied to survey control

#### Equipment Selection

Choosing the right equipment for grading isn't just about what you own—it's about matching the tool to the job.

**Small Residential Driveways (500-2,000 sq ft)**:
- Skid steer or small excavator for excavation
- Hand tools or small plate compactor
- May not need a grader at all

**Parking Lots (10,000-50,000 sq ft)**:
- Excavator for bulk excavation
- Dozer or grader for finish grading
- Vibratory smooth drum roller for compaction
- GPS or laser systems if available

**Large Commercial Sites or Roads (>50,000 sq ft)**:
- Multiple excavators for production
- Motor grader for finish work
- Large rollers for compaction
- GPS machine control is cost-effective at this scale

**Business Tip**: If you don't own the right equipment for a particular job, rent it or subcontract that portion. Using the wrong equipment is inefficient and leads to poor quality. A grader operator can grade in one day what might take three days with a dozer.

#### Grade Control and Tolerances

This is where precision matters. You're creating the foundation for everything else, and if your grades are off, all the careful work that follows won't matter.

**Typical Tolerances**:
- **Subgrade elevation**: ±0.05 feet (about ±½ inch)
- **Base surface elevation**: ±0.03 feet (about ⅜ inch)
- **Final pavement surface**: ±0.02 feet (about ¼ inch)

These might seem like tight tolerances, but they're achievable with proper equipment and technique.

**Why Tolerance Matters**: 
- If your subgrade is 2 inches low in spots, that's 2 inches of extra asphalt or base you're donating to the customer (eating your profit)
- If it's high, you either have to cut it down (extra work) or end up with thin spots in your pavement (durability problems)
- Inconsistent grades mean inconsistent pavement thickness—the thin spots fail first

**How to Achieve Good Grade Control**:
1. **Set accurate benchmarks** before starting work
2. **Check elevations frequently** during grading (every 50 feet minimum)
3. **Use grade stakes or laser guidance** to guide equipment operators
4. **Make multiple passes**, checking and adjusting
5. **Finish with fine grading** to remove ruts and smooth irregularities

**Proof the Grade**: Before moving on, proof roll (drive over with a loaded truck) to verify stability. We'll cover this in detail below.

#### Achieving Design Elevations

Your plans specify design elevations at various points. Here's how to hit them consistently:

**Start from Known Points**:
- Property corners with survey data
- Existing pavement edges or curbs
- Benchmark monuments
- Building foundations

**Work Outward Systematically**: Don't jump around. Grade one section completely, verify it, then move to the next.

**Account for Layer Thicknesses**: 
- If finished pavement elevation is 100.00 feet
- And you have 3 inches of asphalt and 6 inches of base
- Then subgrade elevation should be 99.25 feet (9 inches = 0.75 feet below finished)

**Document Everything**: Take photos, record elevations at grid points, keep daily logs. If there's ever a question about whether you met grade, you have evidence.

**Customer Communication**: If existing grades mean you'll need significant fill or cut, discuss this with the customer before starting work. Unexpected rock excavation or the need for several feet of fill can change project costs significantly.

## 4.4 Asphalt Paving Operations

This is where everything comes together. You've prepared the site, built a solid foundation, and now you're placing the asphalt. This is the visible part of your work, what customers see and judge you by. It's also technically challenging because you're working with a material that's cooling from the moment it leaves the plant, and you have a limited time window to place and compact it.

Excellence in paving operations separates the professional contractors from the amateurs. Let's make sure you're in the first category.

### 4.4.1 Mix Production and Transportation

The paving process starts at the plant, not at the job site. Understanding plant operations and transportation logistics helps you plan better and troubleshoot problems.

#### Asphalt Plant Operations

**Batch Plants vs. Drum Mix Plants**:

**Batch Plants**:
- Produce asphalt in discrete batches (typically 3-6 tons per batch)
- Aggregate is dried and heated, then mixed with binder in a pugmill
- Each batch can be precisely controlled
- Better for specialty mixes or small quantities
- Slower production (150-200 tons per hour typical)

**Drum Mix Plants**:
- Continuous production process
- Aggregate and binder are mixed in a rotating drum
- Higher production rates (300-500+ tons per hour)
- More efficient for large projects
- Most modern plants are drum mix

**Why You Care**: Drum plants can keep trucks moving faster with continuous production. Batch plants may have waiting time between batches. Plan your logistics accordingly.

#### Quality Control During Production

Good quality starts at the plant. Here's what should be happening:

**Material Storage and Handling**:
- Aggregates stored in separate bins by size
- Aggregates kept clean (no contamination with dirt or other materials)
- Asphalt binder tanks maintained at proper temperature
- RAP (recycled asphalt) stored separately if used

**Temperature Control**:
- Aggregate heated to 300-350°F
- Binder heated to 275-325°F
- Mix discharged at 300-325°F (300-350°F for some mixes)
- Temperature monitored with electronic sensors and recorded

**Proportioning**:
- Correct amount of each aggregate size
- Correct asphalt binder content
- Weigh systems calibrated regularly
- Production records kept for every load

**Visual Inspection**: 
- Mix should look uniform, well-coated, with all particles covered in black binder
- Steam rising from mix is normal (moisture evaporating)
- Excessive smoke or blue smoke indicates overheating
- Brown or gray patches indicate inadequate coating

**Sampling and Testing**: 
- Periodic samples taken from plant production
- Tests for gradation, binder content, temperature
- Volumetric testing (air voids, VMA, VFA)
- Frequency: varies by project size and specification (daily to several times per day)

**Business Relationship**: Have a good relationship with your plant. Communicate your job requirements: haul distance, placement rate, any special conditions. Good plants will adjust production to help you succeed.

#### Temperature Monitoring

Temperature is everything in asphalt paving. From the moment mix leaves the plant, it's losing heat. You're in a race against thermodynamics.

**Critical Temperatures** (repeating from Chapter 2 because they're that important):
- **Leaving plant**: 300-325°F minimum
- **Arriving at site**: Above 260°F preferred, 240°F minimum acceptable
- **Behind paver**: 220-290°F
- **Compaction cessation**: 185°F (below this, no further compaction possible)

**Temperature Loss Rate**: 
- Depends on air temperature, wind, haul distance, lift thickness
- Typical: 10-25°F per hour in moderate conditions
- Faster cooling in cold, windy, or thin-lift conditions
- Slower cooling in hot weather or thick lifts

**Measuring Temperature**:
- **Infrared thermometer**: Point and shoot, instant reading. Check multiple spots per load.
- **Dial thermometer**: Insert into mix, wait for stable reading. More accurate for internal temperature.
- **Thermal imaging camera**: Shows temperature distribution, identifies cool spots. Expensive but very useful.

**Documentation**: 
- Record temperature of every load arriving on site
- Record mat temperature during paving and compaction
- If a load arrives below minimum temperature, reject it or note it and use in less critical area

**Temperature Problems**:
- **Load arrives cold** (<240°F): Poor compaction, possible tender mix (depends on binder type)
- **Load arrives too hot** (>350°F): Binder is aging, potential for tender mix, safety concerns
- **Wide temperature variation** within a load: Segregation problem, uneven compaction

### 4.4.2 Paving Equipment and Operation

The asphalt paver is the heart of your paving operation. Understanding how pavers work and how to operate them properly is critical.

#### Asphalt Pavers

**Tracked vs. Wheeled Pavers**:

**Tracked Pavers** (Most Common):
- Ride on rubber or steel tracks like a bulldozer
- Better traction and stability
- Don't mark up underlying surface
- Better for highway and heavy-duty work
- Can work in soft conditions

**Wheeled Pavers**:
- Ride on pneumatic tires
- More maneuverable
- Faster transport between jobs
- Better for parking lots and urban work
- Good ride quality (less operator fatigue)
- Can be problematic on soft bases

**For Most Contractors**: Unless you're doing a lot of highway work, wheeled pavers are fine and more versatile.

#### Major Components

Understanding the major systems helps you operate and troubleshoot:

**1. Hopper**:
- Receives material from truck
- Holds 10-25 tons typically
- Folding doors that open to receive loads
- Bottom is actually a conveyor system

**2. Slat Conveyors**:
- Move material from hopper to rear of paver
- Speed controlled to maintain consistent flow
- If too fast: material piles up behind screed
- If too slow: paver "starves" and stops flowing material

**3. Tunnel and Augers**:
- Material drops into tunnel system at rear of paver
- Augers (rotating screws) spread material across width of paver
- Augers must run full to prevent segregation
- Extensions allow variable paving width

**4. Screed**:
- The business end that forms and smooths the mat
- Can be hydraulically extended for variable widths
- Heated to prevent mix from sticking
- Angle and height controlled to set mat thickness
- Vibrators or tampers help with compaction and surface finish

**Types of Screeds**:
- Fixed width: Less common, limited to one width
- Extending screed: Most common, hydraulically extends to various widths (typically 8-16 feet)
- High-density screed: Extra compaction effort built into screed design

**5. Grade and Slope Controls**:
- Manual: Operator controls screed height by feel and visual reference
- Mechanical: Following a string line or reference wire
- Sonic: Sensors track existing surface or forms
- Laser: Most accurate, reference to grade stakes or 3D GPS

#### Paver Automation and Grade Control

Modern pavers have sophisticated automated controls that improve accuracy and productivity.

**Manual Operation** (Least Sophisticated):
- Operator judges grade visually
- References grade stakes or string lines
- Requires experienced operator
- Accuracy: ±½ inch at best

**Mechanical Grade Control**:
- Following a physical reference (string line, existing curb)
- Sensors detect position relative to reference
- Controls adjust screed to maintain constant offset
- Accuracy: ±¼ inch

**Sonic Averaging System**:
- Ultrasonic sensors "read" the ground on both sides of paver
- Paver maintains constant elevation relative to ground
- Good for overlays and resurfacing
- Accuracy: ±¼ inch

**Laser Grade Control**:
- Rotating laser establishes reference plane
- Receivers on paver track position relative to laser plane
- Most accurate method for new construction
- Accuracy: ±0.1 inch (⅛ inch)

**3D GPS Machine Control** (Newest Technology):
- GPS on paver references digital design model
- No grade stakes needed
- Can pave complex grades and transitions
- Very accurate: ±0.1 inch
- Expensive but becoming more common

**Business Consideration**: Grade control equipment is expensive but can pay for itself on larger projects through improved accuracy (less wasted material) and reduced rework.

#### Joint Matchers for Longitudinal Joints

When paving multiple lanes, the longitudinal joint between lanes is critical. Joint matchers help produce better joints.

**What They Are**: 
- Mechanical or hydraulic devices that attach to the paver
- Physically hold up the edge of the previously placed lane while paving the adjacent lane
- Creates an overlap that gets compacted together
- Results in a tight, well-bonded joint

**Benefits**: 
- Improves joint density (can achieve 95%+ joint density vs. 85-90% without)
- Reduces water infiltration at joint
- Extends pavement life significantly

**When to Use**: High-traffic roads, critical applications, anywhere you want premium joint quality.

### 4.4.3 Material Flow Management

Consistent material flow is critical for quality. Interruptions in flow cause density variations, segregation, and surface defects.

#### Head of Material Maintenance

The paver should maintain a constant "head" of material in front of the screed at all times.

**What It Is**: 
A rolling wave of mix being pushed along by the paver, typically 4-8 inches deep in front of the screed.

**Why It Matters**: 
- Consistent head of material means consistent compaction by the screed
- Too much material: excess weight causes screed to ride high, resulting in thick mat
- Too little material: starves screed, causes thin spots and surface tearing
- Variable head: results in wave patterns in the finished surface

**How to Maintain**:
- Keep material flowing continuously
- Never let hopper empty completely
- Adjust conveyor speed to match placement rate
- Monitor head of material constantly

#### Minimizing Segregation

Segregation is the separation of coarse and fine particles in the mix. It creates weak spots that fail prematurely.

**Causes of Segregation**:
- Poor mix design (too coarse or too harsh)
- Improper plant operations (cold spots in mix)
- Poor truck loading (large stones roll to edges)
- Truck dumping directly into paver (large stones to center)
- Paver operations (material pushing instead of rolling forward)
- Wide temperature variations in the mix

**Signs of Segregation**: 
- Visible patterns of coarse stone vs. fine material
- Streaks or bands in the pavement
- Areas that cool faster (coarse areas) or slower (fine areas)
- Difficulty achieving uniform compaction

**Prevention**:
- Use a Material Transfer Vehicle (MTV, covered below)
- Minimize drop height when loading trucks
- Maintain full hopper and proper head of material
- Run augers consistently
- Don't stop and start unnecessarily
- Keep screed full-width at all times

#### Paving Speed and Consistency

The speed at which you pave affects quality, productivity, and costs.

**Continuous Operation Requirements**:

Stop-and-go paving produces poor results. Continuous, steady operation is essential.

**Problems with Stopping**:
- Density variations where you stopped and restarted
- Temperature loss during delay
- Possible screed marks or bumps
- Reduced smoothness
- Longitudinal joints form at stops

**Achieving Continuous Operation**:
1. **Match plant production to laydown rate**: Plant should produce slightly faster than you pave so trucks are always available
2. **Have next truck ready**: When paver finishes current load, next truck should be backing up
3. **Efficient truck exchanges**: Truck backs in, dumps, pulls away quickly (should take less than 2 minutes)
4. **Plan for delays**: Have extra trucks in rotation to absorb any problems
5. **Communication**: Cell phones or radios between foreman, paver operator, and plant

**Rule of Thumb**: 
For every mile of haul distance, you need one truck in rotation. For a 10-mile haul, you might need 6-8 trucks to keep one paver fed continuously.

#### Material Transfer Vehicles (MTVs)

MTVs are specialized pieces of equipment that significantly improve paving quality.

**What They Are**: 
A self-propelled vehicle that receives material from the delivery truck, remixes it, and then feeds it into the paver hopper. The truck never touches the paver.

**Benefits**:
1. **Eliminates Segregation**: Remixing action blends coarse and fine particles
2. **Maintains Temperature**: Insulated surge hopper keeps mix hot
3. **Provides Surge Capacity**: Holds 15-25 tons, giving buffer if truck is delayed
4. **Smooths Temperature Variations**: Blends hot and cool loads
5. **Consistent Material Flow**: Feeds paver at steady rate
6. **Protects Paver**: No truck impact or contamination

**When to Use**: 
- Interstate and high-speed roads where smoothness is critical
- Any project where segregation has been a problem
- Long haul distances where temperature loss is a concern
- High-volume production where truck timing is difficult
- Projects with stringent smoothness requirements

**Business Case**: On a $500,000 paving project, renting an MTV for $2,000 per day might add 0.4% to project cost but significantly improve quality and reduce rework risk.

### 4.4.4 Longitudinal Joint Construction

Longitudinal joints are the most common failure point in asphalt pavements, so getting this right matters.

#### Best Practices

**Hot-Side vs. Cold-Side Considerations**:

When paving adjacent lanes, the joint can be approached two ways:
1. **Cold Joint**: Pave first lane, let it cool, then pave second lane against the cold edge
2. **Hot Joint**: Pave second lane while first lane is still hot

**Hot Joint Advantages**:
- Better bonding between lanes
- Higher joint density achievable
- Less water infiltration potential

**Cold Joint Reality**: Most paving is done with cold joints because logistics don't allow hot joints. So we need to do cold joints well.

#### Joint Types

**Butt Joint** (Most Common):
- New lane placed directly against edge of first lane
- Overlap is minimal or none
- Requires excellent edge quality on first lane
- Must be well-tacked and properly rolled

**Notched Wedge Joint**:
- First lane has a beveled or sloped edge
- Second lane overlaps into this notch
- Creates mechanical interlock
- Requires special joint former on paver
- Better density and bonding than butt joint

#### Lift Thickness Requirements (4× NMAS Minimum)

For good joint construction, lift thickness at the joint must be adequate.

**The Rule**: Minimum lift thickness at joints should be 4 times the Nominal Maximum Aggregate Size (NMAS) of the mix.

**Why**: 
- Thinner joints don't compact well
- Large stones prevent proper compaction in thin lifts
- Results in low-density, weak joints

**Examples**:
- ½-inch NMAS mix: Minimum 2-inch lift for good joints
- ¾-inch NMAS mix: Minimum 3-inch lift for good joints

#### Density Targets (Within 2% of Mat Density)

Joint density is typically lower than mat density, but it should be close.

**Target**: Joint density should be at least 92-93% of mat density.

**Example**: 
- If mat density is 94% of Gmm
- Joint density should be at least 92% of Gmm

**Improving Joint Density**:
1. **Tack the joint** as discussed earlier
2. **Use proper rolling pattern** (roll joint from hot side first)
3. **Use joint matcher** on paver if available
4. **Notched wedge joint** rather than butt joint
5. **Hot joints** when possible
6. **Adequate lift thickness** (4× NMAS minimum)
7. **Proper temperature management**

## 4.5 Compaction Operations

The best mix in the world is worthless if you don't compact it properly. Compaction is where placement becomes pavement. This is your last chance to create a dense, durable, impermeable surface.

### Compaction Equipment

#### Steel Wheel Rollers (Smooth Drum, Vibratory)

**Static Steel Wheel Rollers**:
- Heavy, smooth steel drum(s)
- No vibration
- Weights: 3-15 tons (larger for production work)
- **Use**: Finish rolling for smooth surface, proof rolling

**Vibratory Steel Wheel Rollers**:
- Same as static but drum vibrates
- Vibration frequency: typically 30-50 Hz
- Amplitude: distance drum moves, adjustable on better rollers
- Modes: most can operate in static or vibratory mode

**How Vibration Works**:
- Rapid up-down movement causes aggregate particles to settle into denser arrangement
- Reduces number of passes needed
- More effective than static weight alone

**When to Use Vibration**:
- Breakdown rolling (first rolling passes)
- Intermediate rolling in temperature range
- NOT for final finish rolling (causes surface checking)
- NOT when mat is too hot (causes shoving)

#### Pneumatic Tire Rollers

**Description**: 
- Multiple rubber tires (typically 4-5 on small units, 9-11 on large units)
- Tires arranged in two rows with offset pattern
- Ballasted (filled with water or sand) to achieve desired weight
- Tire pressure adjustable

**How They Work**:
- Kneading action from tires works mix from bottom up
- Each tire path overlaps, ensuring full coverage
- Rubber conforms to aggregate, creating face-to-face contact
- Seals surface by pressing fines into voids

**When to Use**:
- Intermediate rolling phase
- After breakdown rolling, before finish rolling
- Especially good for fine-graded surface mixes

#### Tandem Vibratory Rollers

Modern tandem vibratory rollers (two drums, both can vibrate) are the most versatile and commonly used rollers in asphalt work.

**Features**:
- Front and rear smooth drums
- Either or both drums can vibrate
- Static weight: 4-14 tons typical
- Variable frequency and amplitude on better models
- Articulated steering for maneuverability

**Operating Modes**:
1. **Both drums vibrating**: Maximum compaction for breakdown rolling
2. **Front or rear only vibrating**: For fine-tuning compaction
3. **Static mode**: For finish rolling

**Most Common Pattern**: 
- Breakdown rolling: rear drum vibrating, front static
- Intermediate: both vibrating or both static depending on temperature
- Finish: both static

### Rolling Pattern and Procedures

How you roll the mat is as important as what equipment you use. The rolling pattern must be completed while the mat is still hot enough to compact.

#### Breakdown, Intermediate, and Finish Rolling

**Breakdown Rolling** (Initial Compaction):
- First rolling passes, starting immediately behind paver
- Temperature: 220-290°F
- Equipment: Vibratory steel drum roller, with vibration on
- Purpose: Achieve most of the compaction quickly
- Typical: 2-4 passes in vibratory mode
- Achieves: 85-92% of target density

**Intermediate Rolling** (Secondary Compaction):
- Following breakdown rolling
- Temperature: 185-220°F
- Equipment: Pneumatic tire roller, or vibratory in static mode
- Purpose: Continue compacting, seal surface
- Typical: 2-4 passes
- Achieves: 92-95% of target density

**Finish Rolling** (Final Smoothing):
- Final passes for surface finish
- Temperature: 185-200°F
- Equipment: Static steel drum roller (no vibration)
- Purpose: Remove roller marks, achieve final smooth surface
- Typical: 1-2 passes
- Achieves: Final surface texture

#### Temperature Windows for Each Phase

Temperature is critical. Roll too hot and you shove the mat. Roll too cold and you don't get compaction.

**Breakdown Rolling**: 
- Start ASAP behind paver
- Ideal range: 230-290°F
- Don't start if mat is so hot it shoves (typically above 300°F)

**Intermediate Rolling**: 
- After breakdown, before mat cools too much
- Ideal range: 185-220°F

**Finish Rolling**: 
- After intermediate compaction
- Ideal range: 185-200°F
- Above 200°F: may leave roller marks
- Below 175°F: surface may check or crack if rolled

**Compaction Cessation Temperature**: 
**185°F is the magic number**. Below this temperature, the mix won't compact any further.

#### Number of Passes Required

This varies with mix characteristics, roller type, and conditions.

**Typical Pattern**:
- **Breakdown**: 2-4 passes vibratory
- **Intermediate**: 2-4 passes pneumatic or static steel
- **Finish**: 1-2 passes static steel
- **Total**: 5-10 passes often achieves target density

**Diminishing Returns**: 
- First few passes achieve most compaction
- Additional passes give smaller improvements
- After 6-8 passes, you're unlikely to get much more density

#### Edge and Longitudinal Joint Rolling Techniques

Edges and joints need special attention because they're vulnerable to low density.

**Edge Rolling**:
- Roll edges first, before interior
- Overlap wheel onto uncompacted mat by 6 inches
- Roll parallel to edge for stability
- May need multiple passes
- Check density at edges (often lower than interior)

**Longitudinal Joint Rolling** (Critical!):
- Roll joint while both lanes are still hot if possible (hot joint)
- For cold joints:
  1. Roll from the **hot side** first, with 6 inches of wheel on new hot mat and 6 inches on cold mat
  2. Roll pattern moves toward cold lane
  3. Several passes along joint before rolling interior
  4. Finish with passes perpendicular to joint

**Why This Pattern Works**: 
- Hot lane is more compactable
- Rolling from hot side pushes material toward the confined (cold) edge
- This increases joint density

**Common Mistake**: Rolling joints from the cold side, which creates low-density joints that fail prematurely.

### Density Achievement

Density is the objective of all this compaction work.

#### Target Density Specifications (93-96% of Gmm)

**Gmm**: Maximum theoretical specific gravity, measured in the lab (ASTM D2041).

**Field Density Target**: Typically 93-96% of Gmm, which means 4-7% air voids in the compacted pavement.

**Why Not 100% Gmm?** 
- Impossible to achieve (would require zero air voids)
- Mix needs some air space to resist rutting

**Typical Specifications**:
- **Surface courses**: 93-95% Gmm (4-7% air voids)
- **Intermediate courses**: 93-96% Gmm
- **Base courses**: 95-97% Gmm (3-5% air voids)

#### Nuclear Gauge vs. Core Testing

**Nuclear Density Gauge** (Most Common Field Test):
- Radioactive source emits gamma rays into pavement
- Detector measures how many rays pass through
- Fast (2-4 minutes), non-destructive
- **Advantages**: Fast, can test at multiple depths
- **Disadvantages**: Requires licensed operator, requires calibration
- **Accuracy**: ±1-2% typically

**Core Testing** (Most Accurate):
- Drill cylindrical core from pavement
- Measure dimensions and weight, calculate density
- Can also measure thickness, binder content, gradation
- **Advantages**: Most accurate, provides sample for additional testing
- **Disadvantages**: Slow, destructive (leaves holes), expensive
- **Use**: Verification testing, dispute resolution, final acceptance

#### Joint Density Requirements (Minimum 90% TMD)

**TMD**: Target Mean Density

**Joint Density**: Density measured at longitudinal joints.

**Typical Requirement**: Minimum 90-92% of TMD
- If TMD is 93% Gmm, joint density must be at least 83.7-85.6% Gmm

**Reality**: Joints are harder to compact than the mat. Expect joint density to be 2-5% lower than mat density even with good practices.

#### Temperature-Density Relationships

**Key Principle**: Density increases as temperature decreases, up to a point.

**What Happens During Compaction**:
1. **At 280-300°F**: Mix is tender, easy to shove
2. **At 250-280°F**: Mix compacts well, vibratory rolling most effective
3. **At 220-250°F**: Continued compaction, pneumatic rolling effective
4. **At 185-220°F**: Final compaction, binder stiffening
5. **Below 185°F**: Binder is too stiff, no further compaction

**The Race Against Time**: 
From the moment the mat is placed, it's cooling. You have a limited window (typically 15-30 minutes depending on conditions) to complete all compaction.

**Thin Lifts in Cold Weather = Very Difficult**: 
A 1.5-inch lift in 50°F weather might cool to 185°F in 10-12 minutes. That's barely enough time for minimal compaction.

**Solutions for Difficult Conditions**:
- Increase lift thickness to retain heat longer
- Use warm-mix technology
- Increase rolling efficiency (more or larger rollers)
- Increase mix temperature (within limits)
- Place during warmest part of day

## 4.6 Weather and Seasonal Considerations

Mother Nature doesn't care about your paving schedule. Weather affects every aspect of asphalt construction.

### 4.6.1 Temperature Requirements

#### Ambient Air Temperature

**Minimum: 50°F (10°C) and Rising**:
- This is the standard minimum for most paving specs
- "And rising" means temperature should be increasing, not decreasing

**Why 50°F Minimum?**:
- Below 50°F, asphalt cools too rapidly
- Compaction window becomes very short
- Difficult to achieve proper density

**Wind Considerations**:
- Wind dramatically accelerates cooling
- A 15 mph wind can double the cooling rate
- Some specs prohibit paving when wind exceeds 25 mph

#### Cold Weather Paving: 32-50°F with Special Provisions

Sometimes you need to pave when temperatures are marginal.

**Special Provisions for Cold Weather Paving**:

1. **Increase Mix Temperature**: Place mix at 300-325°F instead of 275-300°F
2. **Increase Lift Thickness**: Thicker lifts retain heat longer
3. **Use Warm-Mix Technology**: Lower compaction temperature requirements
4. **Insulate Trucks**: Tarps or insulated covers
5. **Minimize Haul Distance**: Cold weather is not the time for long hauls
6. **Increased Rolling Effort**: More rollers or larger rollers
7. **Work During Warmest Part of Day**: 10 AM to 3 PM typically warmest

**When to Just Say No**: 
If temps are at or below freezing, paving is very risky. Your reputation isn't worth the risk.

#### Ground Temperature

**Minimum: 50°F (10°C)**:
Ground temperature is as important as air temperature.

**Why Ground Temperature Matters**:
- Cold ground pulls heat from the asphalt mat very quickly
- Asphalt cools from the bottom up
- Result: good top density, poor bottom density, premature failure

**Measuring Ground Temperature**:
- Infrared thermometer pointed at base course surface
- Take readings in multiple locations
- Do this first thing in the morning before starting paving

**Early Morning Concerns**:
- Even if air temp is 60°F, ground might still be 40°F from overnight cooling
- Give ground time to warm up before paving

### 4.6.2 Mix Temperature Management

**Delivery: Above 260°F (127°C)**:
This is the minimum acceptable temperature for mix arriving at the job site.

**Laying: 220-290°F (104-143°C)**:
Temperature range during placement.

**Final Compaction: Above 185°F (85°C)**:
The absolute minimum temperature for compaction to be effective.

### 4.6.3 Hot Weather Paving

Hot weather is generally easier for paving than cold weather, but it has its own challenges.

**Mat Overheating Prevention**:
- Mix delivered too hot (>350°F) has excess aging
- Communicate with plant about ambient conditions

**Tender Mix Issues**:
- High ambient temps + hot mix = very soft, tender mat
- Mix shoves easily under rollers
- May need to reduce roller amplitude or use static mode
- May need to delay breakdown rolling slightly

**Worker Safety**:
- Heat stress is a real concern
- Provide water, shade, breaks
- Watch for signs of heat exhaustion

### 4.6.4 Wet Weather Restrictions

Water and asphalt paving don't mix well.

**No Paving During Active Precipitation**:
- Rain, snow, sleet, or even heavy mist—stop work
- Water on the surface prevents proper bonding between layers
- Just don't do it

**Why This Rule Exists**:
Trying to pave in wet conditions leads to:
- Poor adhesion of asphalt to existing surface
- Stripping (water gets between binder and aggregate)
- Low density
- Pavement failure

**Wet Base Course**:
- Compaction is nearly impossible on saturated base
- Water has no compressibility
- Must let base dry before placing asphalt

**Standing Water**:
- Absolutely must be removed before paving
- Puddles will cause pavement failure

## 4.7 Quality Control and Quality Assurance

Everything we've covered in this chapter is about building quality pavements. But how do you know you've achieved quality?

**Quality Control (QC)**: Contractor's responsibility to control quality during construction
**Quality Assurance (QA)**: Owner/agency's responsibility to verify that quality has been achieved

### 4.7.1 Contractor Quality Control (QC)

As the contractor, QC is your responsibility and your protection.

#### QC Plan Requirements

Many specs require a written Quality Control Plan before work begins.

**Typical QC Plan Contents**:
1. **Organization Chart**: Who's responsible for quality?
2. **Personnel Qualifications**: Certifications, training, experience
3. **Equipment List**: Testing equipment, calibration records
4. **Sampling and Testing Frequencies**: What tests, how often?
5. **Acceptance Criteria**: Targets for each test
6. **Laboratory**: Commercial lab or in-house?
7. **Documentation**: Daily reports, test results, temperature logs

**Why You Need a QC Plan**: 
- Required for most public work
- Proves you have a system for ensuring quality
- Protects you if there's a dispute
- Shows professionalism to customers
- Actually helps you build better pavements

#### Plant QC Testing

**Gradation Analysis (Sieve Analysis)**:
- Determines distribution of particle sizes
- Frequency: Typically daily or every 1,000-2,000 tons
- Standard: AASHTO T 27 or ASTM C136

**Asphalt Binder Content (Ignition Oven Method)**:
- Burn off binder, weigh remaining aggregate
- Determines percentage of binder in the mix
- Frequency: Daily or every 1,000-2,000 tons
- Standard: AASHTO T 308 or ASTM D6307

**Volumetric Properties (Air Voids, VMA, VFA)**:
- Compact samples, measure dimensions and weights, calculate voids
- Frequency: Daily or every 1,000-2,000 tons
- Standard: AASHTO T 312

**Maximum Specific Gravity (Gmm, Rice Method)**:
- Measure theoretical maximum specific gravity
- Required for calculating field density
- Frequency: Daily or every 1,000-2,000 tons
- Standard: AASHTO T 209 or ASTM D2041

**Mix Temperature Monitoring**:
- Continuous monitoring at discharge
- Recorded for every load

### 4.7.2 Agency Quality Assurance (QA)

On public projects, the agency performs independent QA testing.

#### Verification Testing

**What It Is**: 
Independent testing by the agency to verify that contractor QC is accurate.

**How It Works**:
- Agency takes their own samples
- Agency performs same tests as contractor
- Results compared to contractor's results

**Allowable Tolerances**: 
- **Asphalt content**: ±0.3% is typically acceptable difference
- **Gradation**: ±3-5% on most sieves
- **Density**: ±2% Gmm

#### Acceptance Criteria

**Pay Factor Calculations**:
- Test results converted to a pay factor (0.90 to 1.10 typical)
- If all tests meet spec: Pay Factor = 1.00 (full pay)
- If some tests exceed spec: Pay Factor >1.00 (bonus)
- If some tests below spec: Pay Factor <1.00 (penalty)

**Percent Within Limits (PWL) Analysis**:
- Statistical method based on mean and standard deviation
- PWL >90%: Usually full pay or bonus
- PWL 70-90%: Reduced pay
- PWL <70%: May require removal and replacement

**Business Implication**: 
Pay factors and PWL can significantly affect your profit. If you're consistently achieving 105% pay factors, that's 5% extra profit.

### 4.7.3 Field Testing and Inspection

#### In-Place Density Testing

**Nuclear Density Gauges** (Most Common):
- Radioactive device measures density
- Fast: 2-4 minutes per test
- **Requirements**: Licensed operator, regular calibration

**Testing Frequency**: 
- Minimum 1 test per 1,000-2,000 sq ft
- Random locations
- More tests in questionable areas

**Non-Nuclear Gauges** (Newer Technology):
- Use electromagnetic waves instead of radiation
- No licensing required
- Becoming more popular

#### Core Sampling and Analysis

Cores are the most accurate way to verify pavement quality.

**When Cores Are Taken**:
- Final acceptance testing
- Verification if density gauge results are questionable
- Dispute resolution

**What Cores Tell You**:
1. **Density**: Most accurate measurement
2. **Thickness**: Actual compacted thickness
3. **Binder Content**: Extract binder, determine content
4. **Gradation**: Recover aggregate, perform sieve analysis
5. **Visual Inspection**: See internal structure

**Frequency**: 
- 1 core per 1,000-5,000 sq yds
- Or per specification requirement

#### Thickness Verification

**Methods**:
- **Cores**: Most accurate, measures actual thickness
- **Ground-Penetrating Radar (GPR)**: Non-destructive, fast, surveys entire project
- **During Construction Observation**: Measure at joints and edges

#### Smoothness Testing

**Profilograph Measurements**:
- Mechanical device measures pavement profile
- Results: Profile Index (inches per mile)
- Specification: Typically PI <5-8 inches/mile

**International Roughness Index (IRI)**:
- Computer analysis of pavement profile
- Results: IRI in inches/mile
- Specification: Typically IRI <100-150 inches/mile

**Why It Matters**: 
- Smooth pavements last longer
- Better ride quality
- Many specs have incentives for smooth pavements

## Conclusion

Chapter 4 has covered the entire construction process from clearing the site through final acceptance testing. These procedures and best practices are the foundation of quality asphalt pavement construction.

**Key Takeaways for Your Business**:

1. **Site Preparation Is Critical**: Cutting corners on clearing, subgrade prep, or base construction will come back to haunt you. Do it right the first time.

2. **Temperature Management Is Everything**: From plant to compaction cessation, you're in a race against time. Plan logistics, coordinate crews, and monitor temperatures continuously.

3. **Compaction Determines Durability**: All the careful work before compaction is wasted if you don't achieve proper density. Use proper equipment, techniques, and testing.

7. **Training and Skill Are Invaluable**: Experienced paver operators, skilled roller operators, knowledgeable foreman—invest in training your crews. Their skill directly affects your profit and reputation.

When you master construction practices, you can confidently tell customers: "We build pavements that last because we follow industry best practices at every step. Here's exactly what we'll do and why it matters." That's how you win bids against competitors who just say "We'll pave your lot for $X."

#### Hot Climate Regions

Hot climates—the desert Southwest, the Deep South, anywhere that sees sustained summer temperatures above 100°F—present the opposite challenge: rutting and bleeding. High temperatures soften asphalt binder, and heavy traffic can literally push the pavement into permanent deformation.

**High-Temperature Binder Selection**: The high-temperature grade in the PG system is what protects against rutting. The higher the number (PG 64, PG 70, PG 76, PG 82), the stiffer the binder at high temperatures. In Phoenix or Las Vegas, you might see specifications for PG 76-XX or PG 82-XX binders.

Here's the business case: "We're using a PG 76-22 binder, which is formulated to resist rutting at pavement temperatures up to 76°C—that's 169°F. Even on the hottest summer days with trucks parked in the sun, your pavement will maintain its structural integrity."

**Rutting Resistance Emphasis**: Rutting—permanent deformation in the wheel paths—is the primary distress in hot climates. Prevention strategies include:
- Proper high-temperature binder selection (as discussed)
- Attention to mix design stability (proper aggregate interlock)
- Adequate compaction during construction (get that density!)
- Consideration of modified binders for high-stress applications

For heavy-traffic areas like truck routes, loading docks, or bus stations, consider specifying stone matrix asphalt (SMA) or polymer-modified binders for enhanced rut resistance.

**Polymer Modification Benefits**: In hot climates, polymer-modified asphalts (PMA) really shine. The polymers increase the effective stiffness at high temperatures while maintaining flexibility. This is especially valuable for:
- Interstate highways and major arterials
- Truck parking areas and loading docks
- Intersections and uphill grades (where trucks brake or accelerate)
- Any high-stress application

PMAs cost more (typically 20-40% premium), but for critical applications, the improved performance justifies the investment. When selling this to customers: "For this truck parking area, we're recommending a polymer-modified mix. Yes, it costs more upfront, but it will resist rutting and last significantly longer under these severe conditions. Over the pavement's 20-year life, you'll save money by avoiding premature replacement."

**Heat-Resistant Aggregate Requirements**: Not all aggregates perform equally in hot climates. You need aggregates with:
- High resistance to polish (maintaining surface friction)
- Good particle shape (crushed, angular particles for better interlock)
- Heat stability (won't soften or break down under sustained heat)
- Low absorption (minimizing binder drain-down in hot conditions)

Your local aggregate suppliers should be able to provide materials tested for hot-climate performance. Make sure your mix designs use appropriate materials.

**Practical Construction Considerations**: Working in extreme heat presents challenges:
- Mix can cool rapidly in hot, dry climates (faster hauling, insulated trucks help)
- Workers need heat stress protection (shade, hydration, frequent breaks)
- Early morning paving may be necessary to avoid peak heat
- Material handling must prevent overheating and premature aging

**Why This Matters to Your Customer**: Rutted, bleeding pavements are both ugly and potentially dangerous. When you specify materials and practices appropriate for hot climates, you're protecting your customer's investment and ensuring the pavement performs as expected throughout its design life.
